## Gap analysis

Goal: establish the distance between the current situation and the requirements, and translate it
into a prioritised action plan. Distinct from an internal audit: a gap analysis is used to build,
an audit to verify.

**Process.**

1. **Scope it.** The intended scope, sites, activities, products and services. Certification sought
   or simple conformity. Deadline. What already exists: is there already a QMS, another
   certification, a sector-specific framework?
2. **Determine applicability.** Clause by clause, what applies. Particular attention to 8.3: an
   organisation that defines product or service specifications is doing design, even if it doesn't
   call it that. Any non-applicability must be justified and must not affect the ability to ensure
   conformity.
3. **Gather evidence.** Existing documents, interviews, observation. What hasn't been seen cannot be
   evaluated.
4. **Evaluate each requirement** against a stable scale — see `deliverables.md`.
5. **State the gap**: exactly what is missing, not "needs improvement."
6. **Prioritise.** Three criteria: criticality for certification, implementation effort,
   dependencies. Some clauses drive others — 4.1, 4.2, and 4.4 feed 6.1, which feeds 8.1; start with
   these.
7. **Cost out the plan**: owner, deadline, resources.

**Recommended sequence** for a QMS built from scratch: context and processes (4) → leadership and
policy (5) → planning (6) → support (7) → operations (8) → evaluation (9) → improvement (10). It
follows the dependency logic, not just the numbering.

## Audit programme (9.2.1)

The programme covers a period — usually a year — and the whole QMS. It defines frequency, methods,
responsibilities, planning requirements, and reporting arrangements.

**Three factors determine frequency,** and the standard explicitly requires them:

- **The importance of the processes.** A process critical to product conformity is audited more
  often than a support process. Auditing everything at the same frequency is a gap, not evidence of
  rigour.
- **Changes affecting the organisation.** A new site, a new tool, a reorganisation, a new market, a
  new key supplier.
- **The results of previous audits.** A process that generated nonconformities gets re-audited
  sooner.

Over a complete cycle, all processes and all applicable clauses must have been covered. Nothing
requires auditing everything every time.

**Competence and impartiality.** An auditor does not audit their own work. In a small structure where
full independence is impossible, document the compensating measures: cross-function audits, an
occasional external auditor, findings reviewed by a third party.

## Audit plan

Per audit: scope (processes, sites, activities), criteria (the applicable clauses of the standard
**and** applicable internal documents), dates and times, auditors, auditees, sequence of interviews,
reporting arrangements.

Send in advance. A surprise audit falls outside clause 9.2 and degrades the quality of the evidence
gathered.

**Prepare questions from the requirements,** not from a generic list. For each clause in scope: what
is the requirement, what evidence would demonstrate it, who to ask.

## Gathering evidence

Four sources, to be cross-checked:

- **Documents** — what the organisation says it does.
- **Records** — what attests that it was done.
- **Interviews** — what people understand and actually do.
- **Observation** — what actually happens on the ground.

The gap between these sources is the most useful information in the whole audit. A perfectly
documented process that no one follows is one finding; an effective undocumented practice is another,
of a different nature.

**Technique.** Open questions first ("how do you handle a complaint?"), then a request for evidence
on a real case ("show me the last one"). Following a trace end to end — an order, a complaint, a
hire — crosses several clauses and reveals breakdowns at the interfaces, which clause-by-clause audits
miss entirely.

Never feed the answer. Never write a finding without having checked it with the auditee before the
closing meeting: a misread piece of evidence produces a false finding, and just one is enough to
discredit the whole report.

## Sampling

An audit cannot be exhaustive. Choose the sample deliberately and state it in the report: number of
items, period covered, selection method.

Combine random selection (representativeness) with selection targeted at higher-risk areas: recent
cases, complex cases, cases that already caused a problem, high-load periods, new hires, new
providers.

A finding based on a sample holds for the sample. Word it that way — "of 5 files examined, 3 did not
include…" — rather than generalising with no basis.

## Classifying findings

| Type | Definition | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Major nonconformity** | A complete absence of a requirement, a systemic failure, or a nonconformity affecting the ability to provide conforming products and services | No internal audit ever conducted; no quality objectives established; no release process in place; a recurring failure never addressed |
| **Minor nonconformity** | A one-off or isolated failure of a requirement that is otherwise implemented | One missing competence record out of twelve files; a management review missing one of its input items |
| **Opportunity for improvement** | Compliant, but a more effective arrangement is possible | Indicators are compliant but barely used for steering |
| **Observation** | A factual finding warranting vigilance, with no proven gap | The imminent departure of the only person who masters a process |

**Rules.** A pile-up of minors on the same requirement becomes a major: it signals a systemic
failure. An opportunity for improvement is never a disguised nonconformity — if the requirement isn't
met, it's a nonconformity, and softening it does the organisation a disservice. Conversely, don't turn
a methodological preference into a gap: the question is always "which requirement is not being met?"

## Writing a finding

Three mandatory elements, in this order:

1. **The requirement** — clause number and restatement. What should have been in place.
2. **The evidence** — what was observed, factual, dated, identifiable, with no person named.
3. **The gap** — how the evidence fails to meet the requirement.

**Example.**

> **Minor NC — Clause 7.2 d)**
> *Requirement:* the organisation shall retain appropriate documented information as evidence of
> competence of persons doing, under its control, work that affects the performance and effectiveness
> of the QMS.
> *Finding:* of a sample of 12 personnel files for positions identified as affecting quality (random
> sample, headcount as of 12 March), 4 files contained no evidence of competence — no diploma, no
> evidence of experience, no role assessment. The positions concerned are in final inspection and
> customer relations.
> *Gap:* evidence of competence is not retained for all relevant persons.

What not to write: "Personnel files are incomplete" (no requirement, no evidence, no gap). "Ms X has
no diploma" (names a person, and conflates diploma with competence — the standard accepts education,
training, **or** appropriate experience).

**Don't propose the fix in the finding.** The auditor states the finding; the auditee determines the
cause and the corrective action. Recommendations, if requested, belong in a separate section of the
report.

## Follow-up

After the closing meeting, the auditee analyses the causes and proposes a correction and a corrective
action (10.2). The standard requires that correction and corrective actions be undertaken **without
undue delay**.

Check three things at closure, in order:

1. The **correction** addressed the occurrence.
2. The **corrective action** addresses the cause, and the search for similar cases elsewhere was done
   (10.2.1 b.3).
3. **Effectiveness** was reviewed (10.2.1 d) — after enough time for it to be observable. Closing on
   the day of implementation demonstrates nothing.

A nonconformity is not closed on a promise of action. It is closed on evidence of effectiveness.

## What an auditor does not do

- Audit their own work.
- State a finding with no evidence consulted.
- Demand what the standard does not require — see the list in `clause-requirements.md`.
- Base a finding on a NOTE or on "should" wording.
- Impose a particular method where the standard leaves the choice open.
- Name individuals in findings.
- Consult and audit simultaneously on the same scope.
- Generalise beyond the sample examined.
