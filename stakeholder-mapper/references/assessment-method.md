# Assessing power and interest: method

How to actually derive the ratings SKILL.md asks for, rather than guessing them.

## Sources of evidence for power

- **Budget control** — who signs off on spend that touches this project, even indirectly.
- **Formal approval authority** — who has to say yes for the project to proceed at each gate.
- **Informal influence** — who do other people check with before committing, even without a formal
  role (a long-tenured engineer whose opinion shapes technical decisions regardless of title).
- **Ability to withhold a needed resource** — a team that must contribute time, data, or access can
  block by simply deprioritising the ask, without ever formally saying no.
- **Track record** — has this person or group blocked or significantly redirected a comparable
  initiative before? Past behaviour is the single best predictor available.

Weak evidence to be wary of: title alone, org-chart proximity to the sponsor, how senior someone
"seems" in meetings.

## Sources of evidence for interest

- **Does the project change their workload** — more work, less work, different work.
- **Does it change their metrics** — a team whose KPIs shift because of this project has high
  interest even if they've said nothing yet.
- **Does it touch their turf** — a system, process, or relationship they currently own.
- **Have they engaged so far** — attended meetings unprompted, asked questions, requested updates.
  Absence of engagement is weak evidence of low interest; it's also consistent with not knowing about
  the project yet, which is a different situation with a different response.

## A scoring approach for Standard/Deep mode

Rather than eyeballing "high" or "low," use a simple structured pass for each stakeholder:

| Question | Weight toward power |
|---|---|
| Can they approve or block a key gate? | High |
| Do they control a needed budget or resource? | High |
| Would others defer to their view on this? | Medium |
| Have they blocked something comparable before? | High |

Score each stakeholder against these and let the pattern place them in a quadrant, rather than
starting from a gut impression of "important person" and working backward to justify it.

## Handling stakeholders you can't directly assess

For a stakeholder in another organisation, a regulator, or someone several levels removed, direct
evidence is often unavailable. In that case:

- Ask someone who has dealt with them before, rather than guessing.
- Default to treating uncertain power as high-power until evidence says otherwise — the cost of
  over-engaging a low-power stakeholder is small; the cost of under-engaging a high-power one who
  blocks the project late is large.

## Recalibrating at a milestone

At each review point (see SKILL.md's non-negotiable on this), re-ask the same evidence questions
rather than just asking "has anything changed?" — the second framing tends to produce "no" by default
even when the underlying facts have shifted (a new sponsor's actual engagement level after month one
looks different from the level assumed at kickoff).
