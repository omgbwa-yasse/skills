# Bias mitigation in hiring

Beyond structuring the interview itself (SKILL.md's core method), specific points in the process
where bias enters unnoticed.

## In the job description

- **Gendered language.** Words like "aggressive," "dominant," "ninja," "rockstar" statistically skew
  applicant pools male; "collaborative," "supportive" skew female. Neither is wrong to use once, but
  a description saturated with one register narrows who applies before an interview ever happens.
- **Unnecessary credential requirements.** A degree requirement for a role where it doesn't predict
  performance screens out capable candidates disproportionately from non-traditional backgrounds.
  Ask, for each requirement: would someone without it genuinely fail at this job, or is the
  requirement a proxy for something else that could be assessed directly?

## In sourcing and screening

- **Resume screening on proxies.** School name, employer prestige, and career gaps correlate with
  bias more than with performance. Where possible, screen on the same competencies the interview
  will assess, not on pedigree signals.
- **Referral-heavy pipelines.** Referrals tend to reproduce the existing network's demographics.
  Not a reason to stop taking referrals, but a reason to actively source outside them too if the
  pipeline is narrow.

## In the interview itself

- **Confirmation-seeking questions.** "You seem like a great fit for our culture, don't you think?"
  invites agreement rather than information. Ask what the candidate values in a work environment
  instead, and compare to the role's actual conditions.
- **Small talk drift.** Rapport-building chat before the structured questions is fine, but if it
  runs long and colours the scoring ("I just liked them"), it has become an unstructured, biased
  input smuggled into a structured process. Keep it brief and don't let it inform the score.
- **Different depth of probing for different candidates.** An interviewer who digs deeper into a weak
  answer from a candidate they already favour (giving them a chance to recover) but takes a weak
  answer from a candidate they don't at face value is applying two different bars. Probe consistently.

## In scoring and decision

- **Post-hoc scoring.** Filling in the scorecard from memory after the interview, rather than during,
  lets impression overwrite the specific answers. Score immediately after each answer if possible.
- **Groupthink in panel debriefs.** The most senior or most vocal panelist's opinion, stated first,
  anchors everyone else's. Have each panelist submit scores independently before the group
  discussion, not after hearing others' views.
- **Moving the bar for a candidate you want.** If a "must-have" requirement gets waived for one
  candidate, ask whether it was ever really a must-have, or check it was waived on the same basis for
  every candidate, not selectively.
