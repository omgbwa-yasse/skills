# Recruitment

Recruit the way a practitioner who knows that gut feeling ("good vibe") is the leading source of
bias and hiring error. The guiding principle: the same questions, in the same order, scored on the
same rubric, for every candidate for a given role — structured interviewing measurably reduces bias
compared with free-form interviewing, because it removes the improvisation that leaves the most room
for unconscious preference. The gap is not marginal: unstructured interviews predict job performance
at a validity coefficient of roughly 0.20, structured interviews scored against a defined rubric
reach roughly 0.51 — the structure itself, not the interviewer's skill, accounts for most of that
difference.

## Before anything else: pick the depth

Match effort to what's at stake in the hire. Three modes:

| Mode | When | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **Rapid** | A quick question — "what should I ask to assess X" — or a two-candidate informal comparison | A handful of behavioural questions or a short comparison, in the conversation. No full scorecard. |
| **Standard** | A real hiring round for a single role | Full job description, structured scorecard with rubric, comparative summary across all candidates. |
| **Deep** | A senior or high-impact hire, a panel of multiple interviewers across several rounds, or a role with a history of mis-hires | Standard plus a documented panel-calibration step (evaluators compare rubric interpretation before live interviews) and a written rationale for the final recommendation suitable for a `decision-memo`. |

## Job description

1. **Start from the actual duties**, not a generic template reused from a previous posting. What the
   person will actually do in the first six months.
2. **Distinguish essential from nice-to-have skills.** An exhaustive "nice-to-have" list discourages
   otherwise qualified candidates, particularly those with less conventional backgrounds — limit the
   list to skills genuinely indispensable for the role.
3. **Inclusive language.** Avoid gendered wording or phrasing that presupposes a particular profile;
   describe the job, not the imagined candidate.
4. **Structure**: title, team context, main duties (5-7 maximum, in order of importance), essential
   vs. nice-to-have skills, what is offered (not just salary — autonomy, growth, working context),
   recruitment process and timeline.

## Structured interview scorecard

1. **Define the competencies to assess before writing the questions**, drawn from the job
   description — every question must tie explicitly to a job requirement, never asked out of habit
   or curiosity.
2. **One behavioural question per competency**, phrased to elicit a lived situation rather than an
   opinion: "Tell me about a time you had to trade off two conflicting priorities," rather than "Can
   you manage priorities?" (a closed question with an obvious expected answer).
3. **A shared scoring rubric**, with levels defined by observable anchors (not just 1-to-5 with no
   anchoring) — so two evaluators can score the same answer comparably.
4. **Same questions, same order, for every candidate for the role.** Improvising mid-interview, even
   with good intentions, reintroduces the bias the structure was meant to remove.
5. **A plural panel where possible.** Several independent evaluators partly neutralise individual
   biases; compare scores after the interview, not during, to avoid influencing each other.
6. **Administrative header on every scorecard.** Candidate name, role, date, interviewer name — a
   scorecard without this basic header cannot be reliably compared to the others once a hiring round
   has more than a couple of candidates.

## Comparative candidate summary

A table by assessed competency, candidate by candidate, with the score and a one-line justification
tied to a fact from the interview — never an overall impression ("good vibe," "less convincing")
with no anchor in a criterion. A summary that cannot justify a scoring gap with an observed fact is
not defensible if challenged.

When the summary must be submitted for a hiring decision above the panel's own authority (a
committee, a budget owner), carry it into a `decision-memo` rather than sending the raw table — the
memo forces the single recommendation the table alone does not make.

## The non-negotiables

**Every evaluation criterion ties back to a job requirement.** A criterion that maps to nothing in
the job description has no place on the scorecard — it is often where an unnamed bias enters.

**No decision on impression alone.** Every recommendation rests on answers scored on the rubric, not
on an undocumented interview feeling.

**The job description and the scorecard match.** A competency assessed at interview that does not
appear in the job description, or the reverse, signals a framing problem to fix before continuing
the search.

## Output

- **In the conversation** — interview questions, quick advice, comparing two profiles.
- **`.md`** — job description, full interview scorecard, candidate summary to keep on file.
- **`.docx`** — job description intended for external publication or formal HR distribution. Read the
  `docx` skill first, then `assets/docx-style-guide.md`.

`assets/table-templates.md` has ready-to-copy tables for the scorecard, rubric anchors, and
comparative summary.

Whatever the format, a Standard or Deep hiring round always produces: a scorecard whose criteria all
trace to the job description, and a comparative summary where every score gap is justified by an
observed fact.

## Working with what the user gives you

**They give you interview notes after the fact, not a scorecard filled in live.** Extract what can be
scored against criteria from the notes, and flag clearly where the notes only support an impression
with no specific behavioural example — don't manufacture a score the notes don't actually support.

**They ask you to compare candidates from very different interview formats** (one structured, one
informal chat). Say so explicitly rather than presenting a falsely comparable table — a structured
score and a gut impression are not the same unit, and a summary that treats them as equivalent
misleads whoever reads it.

**They already have a job description and just want questions.** Don't rewrite the job description
uninvited — derive the competencies and questions from what's there, and only flag a gap in the
description if a duty is stated with no way to assess it at interview.

## A note on limits

This skill produces recruitment documents; it is not employment-law advice. Where a jurisdiction's
rules constrain what can be asked at interview, how candidate data can be stored, or what a job
posting must disclose, say so once and point to the need for local HR or legal guidance rather than
assuming the templates here are compliant everywhere.
