# Tactics at the table

Moves for the "during the negotiation" phase of SKILL.md, expanded.

## Anchoring

The first number stated tends to gravitate the rest of the discussion toward it, even for
experienced negotiators who know the effect exists. Implications:

- If you have good information (a well-developed BATNA, market data), anchor first and anchor at the
  edge of what's defensible — not so extreme it damages credibility, but not conservative either.
- If the other side anchors first with a number that seems extreme, don't simply counter-anchor
  reactively — re-state your own researched position and explain the basis, which resets the
  reference point rather than accepting theirs as the starting line.

## Making and receiving concessions

**Diminishing concessions.** Each concession should generally be smaller than the last — a pattern of
equal or growing concessions signals to the other side that more room remains, and they'll keep
pushing.

**Always attach a reason.** A concession justified by something concrete ("we can move on delivery
date because we've resolved the capacity issue") reads as principled; an unexplained concession reads
as a sign of weak resolve and invites further pushing.

**Trade, don't give.** Per SKILL.md's non-negotiable, every concession pairs with an ask. Even a
small, low-cost ask in return signals the concession wasn't free and preserves the pattern for the
rest of the negotiation.

## Handling a stalemate

- **Split the issue.** A single large point of disagreement (price) can sometimes be broken into
  several smaller ones (price, payment terms, volume commitment, contract length) that create more
  room to trade than the single number allowed.
- **Introduce a new variable.** If the ZOPA looks genuinely narrow on the original terms, a
  non-financial addition (extended support, a pilot period, a different delivery structure) can
  create value neither side had priced in, rather than fighting over the same fixed pie.
- **Propose a contingent agreement.** When the two sides disagree about a future uncertain event
  (will the volume really reach the projected level?), a contingent term — a price that adjusts based
  on actual outcomes — can resolve a disagreement that a fixed number can't.

## Reading pressure tactics from the other side

- **Artificial deadlines** ("this offer expires today") — verify whether the deadline is real before
  reacting to it; a deadline that turns out to be soft on inquiry loses its power immediately.
- **Good cop / bad cop** — recognise the pattern and address the substance with whoever is actually
  empowered to decide, rather than getting drawn into managing the dynamic between the two people.
- **Take-it-or-leave-it framing** — test it once, calmly, by proposing a small adjustment; a genuine
  final offer won't move at all, a tactical one usually will.

## Closing

Once terms are agreed, restate them explicitly before ending the session — verbally or in a short
written recap — so both sides leave with the same understanding of what was agreed. A surprising
number of negotiations unravel not from bad faith but from each side remembering the agreed terms
slightly differently.
