# Developing a BATNA

Fisher and Ury's three-step process, expanded with what makes each step actually executable rather
than theoretical.

## Step 1: list every alternative if no deal is reached

Cast wide before narrowing — at this stage, quantity matters more than quality. For a supplier
negotiation: other vendors, insourcing, delaying the purchase, doing without, a partial solution from
an existing vendor. For a salary negotiation: other offers, staying in the current role, a role at a
different company not yet pursued, freelancing, further training before re-entering the market.

## Step 2: develop the most promising into realistic options

For each alternative worth pursuing, do the work that turns "we could" into "we can, and here's
what it would look like":

- **Other vendor** — get an actual quote, not an assumed one. A BATNA priced from memory or an old
  conversation is weaker than one priced this week.
- **Insourcing** — estimate the real cost and timeline, including the ramp-up time, not just the
  headline labour cost.
- **Another job offer** — get it in writing if at all possible, or at minimum confirm the range and
  timeline directly with the other employer, rather than negotiating against a remembered
  impression of an earlier conversation.

The test for "developed enough": could you execute this alternative starting tomorrow if the
negotiation collapsed today? If the honest answer is "not without a lot more work first," it's not
ready to anchor a reservation point yet.

## Step 3: select the best alternative

Compare the developed options on the same criteria that matter in the actual negotiation (cost,
timeline, risk, relationship value) and pick the one that's genuinely best — not the one that's
easiest to describe or the one that happens to be most flattering to defend.

## Strengthening a weak BATNA before the table

If step 3 produces a weak best option, that's information, not a dead end — it tells you where to
invest before negotiating rather than during:

- Secure a provisional or non-binding commitment from an alternative party (a soft offer, a letter of
  intent) — it doesn't have to be final to strengthen your position, it has to be real.
- Shorten the timeline pressure if possible — a BATNA that takes six months to execute is weaker
  under time pressure than one that could start next week, even if the six-month option is otherwise
  better.
- If no alternative can be strengthened in time, that's itself useful to know: it means the
  reservation point should be set more conservatively, and it argues for more patience at the table
  rather than less.

## Estimating the other party's BATNA

Use the same three steps from their likely perspective — what alternatives do they plausibly have,
which would they realistically develop, which would they pick. Sources of information: what they've
said about deadlines or pressures, public information (a company under financial pressure has a
weaker BATNA in a vendor negotiation than one that isn't), and what a comparable party in their
position would typically have available.

Treat this estimate as provisional and update it during the negotiation itself — see "listen to
refine the estimate" in SKILL.md's non-negotiables.
