# Negotiation preparation

Prepare a negotiation the way a practitioner who knows that leverage is decided before the table,
not during it: preparation is the principal determinant of negotiating power, not verbal skill in
the room.

## Before anything else: pick the depth

Match effort to the stakes of the negotiation. Three modes:

| Mode | When | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **Rapid** | A low-stakes, single-issue negotiation (a minor quote, an informal ask) | BATNA and reservation point in a few lines. In the conversation. |
| **Standard** | A real negotiation with a counterpart — supplier, partner, salary | Full four-pillar preparation: both BATNAs, reservation point and target, estimated ZOPA, ranked arguments and concessions. |
| **Deep** | High-value, multi-issue, or multi-party negotiation, or one where walking away has serious consequences | Standard plus a stakeholder map of everyone influencing the counterpart's position, and the walk-away decision framed explicitly for a `decision-memo` if it needs sign-off above the negotiator. |

## The four pillars

**BATNA (Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement) — my best alternative.** What I do if no
agreement is reached. A strong BATNA shifts my reservation point in my favour; a weak or non-existent
BATNA forces me to accept degraded terms to avoid the negotiation failing. Strengthen it before the
negotiation, not during: secure a concrete alternative offer, a provisional agreement, a fallback
that is executable — not just theoretical.

**Estimated BATNA of the other party.** What they would do if the agreement fails, as best I can
assess it. A counterpart with a weak BATNA has more to lose from failure than I do — knowing this
before the table shifts the balance of power in my favour.

**Reservation point** — the threshold below (or above) which I prefer my BATNA to the deal. Directly
derived from my BATNA: the stronger it is, the more demanding my reservation point can be. Fisher and
Ury (*Getting to Yes*) specifically warn against setting a rigid "bottom line" in advance instead of
grounding this threshold in the BATNA: a bottom line fixed before the discussion is often arbitrary,
and committing to it publicly kills the inventiveness needed to find an option neither side saw
coming. Let the BATNA — not a number chosen for reassurance — set the threshold.

**ZOPA (Zone Of Possible Agreement)** — the space, if it exists, between my reservation point and the
other party's. If the reservation points do not overlap, no agreement is possible as things stand —
knowing this before the table avoids negotiating in a vacuum or conceding unnecessarily while
believing a compromise is still to be found.

`references/batna-development.md` expands Fisher and Ury's three-step process into an executable
checklist, including how to strengthen a weak BATNA before the table and how to estimate the other
party's.

## Building the preparation

1. **My BATNA** — described concretely, with its estimated value, and the actions needed to
   strengthen it before the negotiation.
2. **The other party's estimated BATNA** — based on what I know of their situation, constraints, and
   known alternatives.
3. **My reservation point and my target** — the minimum acceptable threshold, distinct from the
   objective pursued (always more ambitious than the reservation point, to leave real room to
   negotiate).
4. **Estimated ZOPA** — the zone where agreement is possible; if it looks narrow or non-existent,
   think in advance about what could widen it (a non-financial element to add, splitting the issue).
5. **Arguments and evidence**, ranked by strength — not all deployed at once: keep a reserve for
   later in the exchange.
6. **Concessions prepared in advance**, ranked by what they actually cost — knowing before the table
   what can be conceded without harm and what cannot avoids conceding under pressure a point that
   should have held.

## During the negotiation

**Never reveal the reservation point.** The other party will adjust their demand as soon as they
know it.

**Listen to refine the estimate of the other side's BATNA**, not just to respond — what the other
party reveals during the session (deadlines mentioned, constraints raised) sharpens the estimate made
in preparation.

**A concession calls for a counter-concession.** Giving something up without asking for anything in
return signals that further concessions will follow with no effort from the other side.

`references/tactics.md` covers anchoring, diminishing concessions, handling a stalemate, and reading
pressure tactics from the other side — read it before a Standard or Deep negotiation, not during one.

## The non-negotiables

**BATNA must be executable, not merely theoretical.** "We could find another supplier" with no work
underway is not an operative BATNA — it carries no weight in the balance of power while it stays
hypothetical.

## Where this feeds from and into

When several parties are involved and it is not obvious who actually needs to be brought to the
table, map them first with `stakeholder-mapper` — power and interest largely determine each party's
real BATNA. Once the negotiation is prepared, the choice of walking away versus accepting a given
deal is itself a decision: if it needs to be justified to someone above the negotiator, write it up
as a `decision-memo` rather than deciding informally.

**The reservation point is set before the negotiation, never in the room under pressure.** Setting it
on the fly, in the heat of the exchange, reliably produces a lower threshold than a cold-headed
analysis would have justified.

**If no ZOPA exists, say so rather than negotiating anyway.** Pursuing a negotiation with no possible
agreement burns time and relationships for a result that is structurally out of reach — better to
know it and either widen the zone (non-financial options) or postpone.

## Output

- **In the conversation** — full preparation for a routine negotiation.
- **`.md`** — a preparation dossier to keep, especially for a high-stakes or recurring negotiation
  (procurement, partnerships).

`assets/table-templates.md` has ready-to-copy tables for the four-pillar preparation and the
concessions list.

Whatever the format, a Standard or Deep preparation always contains: an executable BATNA (not a
theoretical one) and a reservation point derived from it rather than chosen for reassurance.

## Working with what the user gives you

**They tell you their target price/terms but not their BATNA.** Don't build the preparation around
the target alone — ask what happens if no deal is reached, since the target without a BATNA behind it
is just a wish, not a grounded reservation point.

**They want to know what to say in the room, not a written prep document.** Give the talking points
directly, anchored to the pillars, rather than insisting on the full written format — the four
pillars can live in the conversation as a briefing rather than a document.

**They've already started negotiating and are stuck.** Diagnose first: is there genuinely no ZOPA, or
has the reservation point drifted under pressure mid-negotiation? The fix is different — no ZOPA needs
a widened scope or a pause; a drifted reservation point needs to be re-anchored to the actual BATNA
before continuing.

## A note on limits

This skill produces negotiation strategy; it is not legal advice on the resulting agreement. Where
the negotiation will produce a binding contract, say so once and point to the need for legal review
of the final terms before signature.
