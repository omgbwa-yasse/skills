# Common failure patterns

What makes a memo bounce back unread or unresolved, beyond the non-negotiables already in SKILL.md.

## The buried lede

The recommendation is technically present, but on page 2, after three paragraphs of context. A
reader skimming the first screen sees only background and closes the document assuming it's a status
report, not a decision to make. Fix: recommendation in the first or second sentence, always.

## The false option

An options table with three rows where two are strawmen no reasonable person would pick ("do
nothing and let the problem get worse," "spend 10x the budget for a marginal gain"). This isn't
genuine comparison — it's a recommendation dressed up as an analysis. Decision-makers who've seen
this pattern before recognise it immediately and it costs credibility on every future memo from the
same author. Build options that a thoughtful person could actually choose.

## The uncosted recommendation

"We should invest more in X" with no figure attached. A decision-maker can't approve what they can't
size. If the true cost isn't known yet, say so explicitly and give a range or the plan to find out —
never let cost stay implicit.

## The moving owner

The memo names "the team" or "leadership" as responsible for implementation. Six months later,
nothing happened, and no one is accountable because no one was ever named. This is the single most
preventable failure — always name one function or one person.

## The memo that reopens itself

A memo that ends with "let's discuss further" instead of a decision and a deadline isn't a decision
memo — it's a discussion starter that borrowed the format's authority without doing its job. If the
matter genuinely isn't ready to decide, say that plainly (see "Refusal is a legitimate option" in
SKILL.md) rather than producing a memo that looks decisive but changes nothing.

## The stale memo

Written once, never updated, still circulating after the facts underlying it have changed (a cost
estimate that's a year old, a "current state" that's no longer current). A decision memo has a shelf
life — note the date the analysis was current as of, so a reader six months later knows to ask
whether it still holds.
