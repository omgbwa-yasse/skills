# Professional and administrative writing

Write the way a competent administrator writes: one subject, a plan, facts that can be checked, a register chosen for the relationship, and an ending that leaves no doubt about who does what next.

The failure this skill exists to prevent is the fluent message that does no work — polite, well-formed, and impossible to act on because the ask is buried, the context is missing, or the writer hedged the decision into invisibility. The second failure is its opposite: a blunt message that gets the content right and costs the relationship.

**Language.** Write in English. If the person explicitly asks for French, comply and read `references/french-administrative.md`, which holds the formula repertoire and conventions of the French administrative tradition.

## Before drafting

Three things must be settled. Infer them from the request where possible; ask only what genuinely changes the draft, and never more than three questions.

1. **The purpose.** What must the reader do, decide, know, or feel after reading? A document with two purposes is two documents.
2. **The relationship.** Superior, peer, subordinate, client, supplier, regulator, external body, unknown. This governs register, and register is not decoration — see below.
3. **The document type.** Email, letter, memo, minutes, report. Each has its own shape; see `references/document-types.md`.

Where the person supplies a thread, a prior message, or a file, read it before drafting. Where they supply nothing but a topic, draft from stated assumptions rather than interrogating them.

## The non-negotiables

**One subject per document.** Two unrelated matters in one message means one of them gets lost. Split them and say so.

**Plan before writing.** Every document has three movements: an opening that states the purpose and re-establishes the context; a development that gives the facts, the reasoning, or the regulation; a conclusion that states the decision, the request, or the proposal. Order the development deliberately — chronological, particular to general, facts to opinion, observation to diagnosis, or essential to detail. Choose one and hold it.

**Assume the reader does not have the file.** They may not remember the exchange, may not have been copied, may be reading three weeks later. Restate what is being referred to, briefly. When responding, reformulate the request before answering it — this proves the request was understood and lets the reader confirm.

**Facts, with their references.** Dates, figures, document numbers, names of texts. Nothing asserted that could not be verified if challenged. Where a figure is approximate, say so.

**Never invent.** No fabricated dates, reference numbers, names, prices, article numbers, or regulatory citations — an official document containing invented references is worse than no document. Where the person has not supplied a detail the document needs, insert an explicit placeholder in square brackets — `[date of the meeting]`, `[invoice number]` — and list them under the draft. Never smooth over a gap with a plausible-looking value.

**The conclusion carries the ask.** Who does what, by when, and what happens next. A document that ends without this has not finished. If the answer is a refusal, refuse plainly and give the reason; a refusal the reader has to infer will be read as evasion, then discovered, and resented twice.

**Register is a decision.** Calibrate to the relationship and the stakes, and be able to say why. Default to the neutral professional band; move up or down deliberately. See `references/register.md`.

## Style

The five qualities, in order of how often they are missed: **clear, complete, concise, convincing, correct**.

- One idea per sentence. Two sentences of one clause each beat one sentence of two clauses.
- Every sentence carries new information. Cut any sentence that only restates.
- Prefer plain words. No jargon; expand every acronym at first use.
- Cut subordinate clauses. Balance paragraph lengths.
- Positive formulations over negative ones — say what will happen, not only what will not.
- Avoid the affective tone and the peremptory tone alike. Where softening is needed, use the conditional, a qualifying adverb, or an indirect question rather than an apology.
- No value judgements about people. Describe conduct and consequence, not character.
- Cut redundancy on rereading. Deliberate repetition is rare; accidental repetition signals loose thinking.

`references/mechanics.md` covers punctuation, capitalisation, and the errors that recur.

## Document types

`references/document-types.md` gives the shape, the required elements, and the traps for each: **email**, **formal letter**, **memo / service note**, **transmittal slip**, **meeting minutes**, **formal minutes of record**, **report to a decision-maker**.

Choose the type before drafting. Common mismatches: a report where a memo would do; minutes where a decision record was needed; an email where the matter required a letter that can be filed and cited.

Two adjacent skills cover ground this one deliberately leaves out: `meeting-facilitator` prepares the agenda and runs the live decision log during a meeting — this skill only writes the minutes once it is over. `decision-memo` is the dedicated format when the document's entire job is to force a choice between options with a named owner — a report to a decision-maker written under this skill's own template works for a lighter case, but read `decision-memo` first whenever the ask is explicitly to get something approved or arbitrated.

## Output

Decide per request; do not default.

- **In the conversation** — a short email or message the person will copy out, or a question about tone or phrasing.
- **Two or three variants** — when the strategic choice is real (push versus concede, soften versus hold firm, close the door versus leave it open). Label each by what it achieves, not by its tone, and say what each trades away. Do not produce variants when there is one sound approach; that pushes the decision back onto the person for no reason.
- **`.docx`** — a formal letter, report, or minutes intended to be signed, filed, sent to a client or authority, or circulated as an official record. Read the `docx` skill first.
- **`.md`** — a long document the person will edit further.

After any draft, add a short note only if there is something the person must decide: placeholders to fill, an assumption made, or a risk in the approach. No commentary on the writing itself.

## Reviewing someone else's draft

Read for the non-negotiables in order: one subject, clear purpose, context restated, facts referenced, register appropriate, ask unambiguous. Report what actually weakens the document, then give the corrected version. Do not rewrite wholesale what already works — preserve the person's voice and fix what fails. Where the register is wrong for the relationship, say so explicitly; it is the error people least often see in their own drafts.
