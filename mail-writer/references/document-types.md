# Document types

Choose the type before drafting. Each has a shape, required elements, and a characteristic failure.

**Contents:** [Choosing](#choosing-the-type) · [Email](#email) · [Formal letter](#formal-letter) · [Memo / service note](#memo--service-note) · [Transmittal slip](#transmittal-slip) · [Meeting minutes](#meeting-minutes) · [Formal minutes of record](#formal-minutes-of-record) · [Report](#report-to-a-decision-maker) · [Meeting notice](#meeting-notice)

## Choosing the type

| The situation | Type |
|---|---|
| Routine exchange, quick decision, ongoing thread | Email |
| Must be filed, cited, signed, or sent to an external authority | Formal letter |
| Internal instruction, information, or summary for a colleague or superior | Memo / service note |
| Documents being transmitted with no commentary needed | Transmittal slip |
| Record of what a meeting discussed and decided | Meeting minutes |
| Record of a body with decision-making power, subject to approval | Formal minutes of record |
| Analysis of a problem leading to a recommended decision | Report |
| Calling people to a meeting | Meeting notice |

**Frequent mismatches.** A report where a memo would do — a decision-maker asked for a page and received fifteen. Minutes where a decision record was needed — the discussion is preserved and the decisions are not. An email where a letter was required — the matter later needs to be cited and only a thread exists.

## Email

**Shape.** Subject line · salutation · purpose in the first sentence · context · body · ask · closing · signature.

**The subject line is a working element,** not a label. It should let the reader decide priority and find the message again in six months. *Revised delivery schedule — confirmation needed by 14 March* beats *Update*. One subject per email; a second matter is a second email with its own subject line.

**The first sentence states the purpose.** The reader should know why they are reading before they reach the second paragraph. Context follows the purpose; it does not precede it.

**Length.** If the message exceeds roughly a screen, ask whether it should be a memo or a report attached to a short email. Long emails get skimmed and the ask gets missed.

**Reply-specific.** Reformulate the request before answering it. When answering several questions, mirror the sender's order and mark each — numbered points, or short bold leads. When declining, put the decision in the first or second sentence, then the reason.

**Recipients.** *To* is for people who must act. *Cc* is for people who must know. Copying a manager to apply pressure is visible to everyone and remembered; if the escalation is warranted, do it explicitly instead.

**Failure mode.** The ask buried in paragraph four, or absent — the reader finishes the message informed and does nothing.

## Formal letter

For anything that must be signed, filed, cited, or sent to an external authority, a client at arm's length, or a regulator.

**Elements, in order:**

1. **Sender block** — organisation, service, address, contact details, upper left.
2. **Reference** — your file reference and, where relevant, theirs. Enables retrieval and citation.
3. **Contact** — name, direct line, or email of the person handling the matter.
4. **Place and date** — upper right, date written in full.
5. **Recipient block** — name, title or function, full address. Where the matter is official, address the responsible authority by function, not a named individual in a subordinate position.
6. **Subject** — one line, one matter.
7. **Enclosures** — number and nature, noted at the foot of the first page.
8. **Salutation.**
9. **Opening** — states the matter completely and briefly, restating what the reader needs to understand it.
10. **Development** — the facts, the argument, the applicable rules. Ordered progressively, from the less decisive to what justifies the conclusion. Balanced parts, with transitions.
11. **Conclusion** — the decision, the answer, or the proposal, stated without ambiguity, whether it is affirmative, negative, or an interim response.
12. **Closing formula** — matching the salutation.
13. **Signature** — name, function. Where signing on behalf of someone else, the basis must appear: by delegation, by authorisation, or for and on behalf of, with the signatory's own name and title.

**Chain of authority.** In hierarchical organisations, a letter to a higher authority passes through the immediate superior — which informs the intermediate levels and lets them add their view. This is a routing requirement, not a courtesy. Exceptions exist for genuinely urgent matters, where the original goes through the chain and a copy goes directly, marked as such.

**Failure mode.** Written as an extended email: no reference, no subject line, no filing handle, and unusable as a citable record.

## Memo / service note

Internal. Brief and concentrated. Three directions of travel:

- **Upward** — summarise a file, report on a question, or prompt a decision.
- **Downward** — give instructions or announce an official arrangement affecting the service.
- **Lateral** — inform another service, or accompany a document with the explanation it needs.

**Elements.** Sender block · subject · reference · addressee line (*Memo to:* followed by title) · date · body · signature.

**Types worth distinguishing**, because they call for different tone and structure:

| Type | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Service note | Inform staff of an official arrangement affecting how the service operates |
| Covering note | Accompany a file being transmitted |
| Explanatory note | Add precision to a matter already dealt with |
| Corrective note | Record errors or failures to comply |
| Synthesis note | Draw a clear, coherent account from diverse and possibly conflicting sources, to enable a decision |

**Length.** A memo that runs past two pages is a report wearing the wrong label.

**Failure mode.** The corrective note written in the heat of the moment. Describe the conduct and its consequence; never the person's character. It will be kept.

## Transmittal slip

For moving documents between services where no commentary is needed and a covering letter would be overkill.

**Structure.** Standard correspondence header, then a three-column table: **document · number of copies · observations**. The sender keeps a duplicate, which is the point — it records what was sent and to whom, and where an original now sits.

**The observations column carries the instruction,** and each phrase has a settled meaning:

| Phrase | Meaning |
|---|---|
| For action | The sender relinquishes the file; the recipient decides the matter |
| For information | No action expected; the recipient may keep the documents |
| For whatever use may be made of it | The recipient is free to act or not |
| On loan / for review and return | The recipient reads and returns the document |
| For follow-up | The recipient must act, but need not report back |
| For opinion | The recipient must give a reasoned view |
| For signature / for approval | Sent to an authority to sign or endorse |
| For input to a reply | The recipient supplies information enabling the sender to answer |
| Copy to | List of further recipients |

Using these loosely defeats the instrument: *for information* and *for follow-up* create entirely different obligations.

## Meeting minutes

An account of what happened. Serves as the memory of the group and can be relied on later to establish what took place.

**Two kinds.** *Full minutes* reproduce the content of a session — appropriate for a lecture, a hearing, a formal debate. *Selective minutes*, or a record of conclusions, capture the decisions and the information worth retaining, selected against the purpose and the reader's needs. Selective is right for most working meetings.

**Opening block:** date, place, time · subject or agenda · name and function of the chair · names and functions of those present · names of those absent with apologies or represented.

**Body — choose the plan deliberately:**

- **Chronological** — events in the order they occurred. Right when sequence matters: an incident, a negotiation, a hearing.
- **Thematic** — organised by subject. Right for most meetings, because it drops the digressions and repetitions that any discussion contains.

**Closing.** Note the time the meeting ended. Where a vote was taken, record its nature (show of hands, secret ballot) and its result (votes for, against, abstentions).

**Discipline.** Maximum objectivity, even though selection is unavoidable. No judgement, no interpretation, no attribution of motive. Record decisions with their owner and deadline — minutes without owners produce meetings that repeat themselves.

**Failure mode.** Recording the discussion and losing the decisions. The reader in three months needs what was decided, by whom, and by when.

## Formal minutes of record

Distinct from meeting minutes, and the distinction matters. Formal minutes are mandatory for bodies with decision-making power — a board, a governing council, a statutory committee. They are drawn up by a designated secretary, and they must be approved by the participants, usually at the following session, before they are valid.

Concise and precise, containing no judgement and no view of their author. They record a fact whose trace must be preserved in the interest of a community, and they can be relied on in a dispute.

**Required elements.** Name and date of the meeting · place · name of the body · names and functions of participants · agenda · every question put to a vote and its result (for, against, abstention) · approval status.

**A separate use:** minutes of finding, describing a fact or event that might later be contested. Legal action may be founded on these. Purely descriptive, dated, signed.

Formal minutes are official documents. File them chronologically by category.

## Report to a decision-maker

Distinct from a memo, which briefly sets out routine matters, and from minutes, which describe what happened. A report starts from the study of a problem or the analysis of a situation, and produces a reasoned opinion and a proposed course of action. It is a demonstrative document whose purpose is to enable a decision.

**Elements.** Sender block · date and place · subject (sometimes carried in the title) · reference · a sender line stating the function of both the author and the addressee · body · date and signature of the author. A substantial report is preceded by a covering letter.

**Structure:**

- **Introduction** — the facts or instructions that prompted the report, and its object. Where the subject requires it, a genuine statement of the situation. As concise as the subject allows.
- **Development** — parts adapted to the case. A common and sound pattern: the facts; then their consequences; then the possible courses of action examined in turn, with what each would cost and achieve.
- **Conclusion** — the proposed decision. It introduces no new information and does not summarise what precedes it. Where approval is sought, a closing formula inviting it is appropriate.

**Qualities, in the order they are tested:** *objective* — nothing asserted that cannot be justified, founded only on verified data. *Complete* — no aspect of the question omitted, including the arguments against the recommendation. *Synthetic* — the various elements presented as a coherent whole rather than a pile of findings.

Where possible, the report answers: who, what, where, when, how — and arrives at clear proposals. Even though a report normally involves the author taking a position, the reader must be able to choose between the options presented without difficulty. A report that makes only one option viable has not analysed; it has advocated, and a decision-maker will notice.

**Failure mode.** Recommending without costing the alternatives, so the reader cannot test the recommendation and must either accept it on trust or send it back.

## Meeting notice

Calls people to a meeting. Takes the form of a short letter or a memo.

**Required:** subject of the meeting · agenda · date and time · exact location · who will chair · who is invited. Add any preparatory work required or documents to bring.

**Note on wording.** One *summons* downward and *invites* otherwise. Summoning a peer or a superior is a register error, and a conspicuous one.
