# Register: calibrating formality to the relationship

Register is a decision about the relationship, not a politeness setting. The French administrative tradition this skill draws on encodes hierarchy directly into verb choice; English encodes it through modality, directness, and hedging. The underlying logic transfers: **what you may ask for, and how directly, depends on where you stand relative to the reader.**

## The five bands

| Band | When | Marker |
|---|---|---|
| **Formal / official** | Regulators, courts, ministries, formal notices, anything that may be cited or filed | Full titles, no contractions, third-person constructions available, complete closing formula |
| **Formal professional** | Senior external parties, clients at arm's length, first contact, sensitive matters | Full name and title, no contractions, measured modality |
| **Neutral professional** | The default. Colleagues across teams, established clients, suppliers, most business email | First name where the relationship allows, light contractions, direct but courteous |
| **Collegial** | Close colleagues, established working relationships, internal teams | Contractions, shorter sentences, minimal ceremony |
| **Brief internal** | Same team, ongoing thread, operational matters | No opening or closing formula, straight to the point |

Move one band up when the matter is sensitive, when the relationship is new, when the document may be read by people beyond the addressee, or when it will be filed. Move one band down when over-formality would itself signal distance or displeasure — a sudden shift to full formality with a close colleague reads as a rebuke, sometimes usefully, but never accidentally.

## The hierarchical axis

Independent of formality band, position governs what may be asked and how.

**Writing upward** (to a superior, a client, an authority, an elected official): you report, submit, propose, and request. You do not instruct. Use the conditional and the modal past — *I would be grateful if*, *it would be helpful if*, *may I suggest*, *I should be glad to*. Signal that the decision is theirs. Where you need something, ask; do not schedule it for them.

- report to · submit for your consideration · set out for you · request your approval · seek your guidance · propose that

**Writing across** (to a peer, another service, an external partner of equal standing): direct requests are appropriate; instructions are not. The conditional remains useful where you are asking for something outside their obligation to you.

- inform you that · draw your attention to · confirm that · ask that · propose that · suggest we

**Writing downward** (to a subordinate, a supplier under contract, a service you direct): the present indicative is available and appropriate. Instructions can be stated as instructions. Softening is still normal courtesy, but hedging an instruction until it reads as optional is a failure — the reader will treat it as optional.

- inform · advise · require that · ask you to · direct that · expect that

**The trap in both directions.** Writing upward with downward verbs reads as presumptuous. Writing downward with upward verbs produces instructions nobody follows, and then a second message that has to be sharper than the first would have needed to be. When in doubt about a lateral relationship, write across, not up.

**The functional-authority case.** Where you have no statutory authority over the reader — writing to an elected official, a board, a partner organisation, or a client who pays you — use the conditional regardless of your own seniority. Position in your own hierarchy does not transfer outside it.

## Openings

**Referring to something prior.** These do the work of restating context in one clause:

- Further to your email of 12 March · In response to your request of · Thank you for your letter of · I refer to our conversation of · As agreed at the meeting of · Following your enquiry regarding · You raised with me the question of · It has been brought to my attention that

**Opening a new matter:**

- I am writing to inform you that · I am writing to request · I would like to draw your attention to · I am writing regarding · The question has arisen as to whether · I am writing to confirm

**Upward variants:** I am writing to report on · I should like to submit for your consideration · I am writing to seek your approval for

**Downward variants:** I am writing to advise you that · This is to confirm the arrangements for · I am writing to remind you that

Avoid opening with an apology (*Sorry to bother you*), with a status report on your own state (*I hope this finds you well* is acceptable filler but does no work), or with the history of the file before the reader knows why they are being written to. State the purpose first, then the context.

## Stating and exposing

Precise verbs, present indicative, for setting out what you assert:

- I confirm · I note · I observe · I consider · I find · I would point out · I would emphasise · I recall that · I add that · I acknowledge

Softer, more tentative:

- It appears that · It would seem that · I understand that · My understanding is that · It is not impossible that · I do not lose sight of the fact that · It should not be overlooked that

Firmer, when a position must be held:

- I must point out that · I cannot but observe that · I take note of · It cannot be the case that · There can be no question of

Use *I* rather than *we* when the responsibility is personally yours. The first person singular marks assumed responsibility; the plural diffuses it, which is sometimes exactly the intention, and should therefore be a choice.

## Transitions

Paragraph connectors carry more of the reader's sense of structure than writers expect:

- first · second · finally
- on the one hand · on the other hand
- not only · but also
- furthermore · in addition · moreover
- however · nevertheless · that said

Weighting an argument or a requirement:

- principally · as a secondary matter · exceptionally · for information only · as a matter of record

Time:

- with immediate effect · without delay · as soon as practicable · by [date] at the latest

*By [date]* beats *as soon as possible*, which sets no obligation and will be read as no urgency.

## Asking

**Upward or to an authority** — the conditional does the work:

- I should be grateful if you would · I would be grateful for your approval of · Would you be willing to · May I ask that · I would welcome your view on · If you agree, I will proceed on that basis

**Across:**

- Could you please · I would ask that · Please let me have · I would appreciate it if you could

**Downward:**

- Please · I ask you to · Please ensure that · You are asked to · I expect that

**Formal or contractual:**

- You are requested to · It is necessary that · It falls to you to · It is incumbent upon you to · I must ask that

Whatever the band, the ask names the actor, the action, and the deadline. *Please confirm by Friday 14 March whether the revised specification is acceptable* does work that *Let me know your thoughts* does not.

## Closings

| Band | Closing |
|---|---|
| Formal / official | Yours faithfully (no name known) · Yours sincerely (name known) |
| Formal professional | Yours sincerely · With kind regards |
| Neutral professional | Kind regards · Best regards · With thanks |
| Collegial | Regards · Thanks · Best |
| Brief internal | (none) |

Before the closing, a final line where it serves: *I remain available should you require any further information* (formal) · *Please do let me know if you need anything further* (neutral) · *Happy to discuss* (collegial).

Do not close with a fresh request. The ask belongs in the conclusion, not tacked after the courtesy.

## Softening and hardening

**To soften without weakening the ask** — change the modality, not the content:

- *You must send the report by Friday* → *I would be grateful to have the report by Friday.* The deadline survives; the imperative does not.
- Use the conditional, an indirect question, or a qualifying adverb.
- Attribute the constraint to the situation rather than to yourself: *the schedule requires* rather than *I need*.
- Acknowledge the reader's position before stating yours.

**To harden without becoming rude** — the escalation ladder, in order:

1. Drop the softeners. Present indicative, no conditional.
2. Name the consequence factually, without threat: *Without confirmation by Friday, the installation will be rescheduled to the next available slot, in June.*
3. Reference the prior exchange: *This is my third request on this matter, following my emails of 3 and 17 March.*
4. Move up a formality band. The shift from *Kind regards* to *Yours sincerely* is itself a signal, and a well-understood one.
5. Widen the circulation, or say that you will.

Never soften a refusal into ambiguity. *We are unable to accommodate this request* followed by the reason is kinder than three paragraphs the reader must decode.

## Diagnosing register errors

Signs the register is wrong, and what they usually mean:

- **Over-hedged instruction to a subordinate** — will not be actioned; you will have to write again, harder.
- **Instruction to a superior or client** — reads as presumptuous regardless of how correct the content is.
- **Sudden formality with a close colleague** — reads as displeasure, whether or not intended.
- **Sustained informality with an external authority** — reads as not taking the matter seriously.
- **Apologising for making a legitimate request** — undermines the request and invites refusal.
- **Formal closing on a two-line internal thread** — reads as stiff, or as a signal something is wrong.
- **Emotive vocabulary in an official document** — the content stops being verifiable, and the document loses its evidential value.
