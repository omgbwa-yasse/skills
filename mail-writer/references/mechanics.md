## Sentences

Every sentence runs from a capital to a full stop and is grammatically complete. This sounds trivial and is the most frequent structural failure in professional writing, where fragments and run-ons proliferate under time pressure.

**Two sentences of one clause beat one sentence of two clauses.** Reduce subordination. Where a sentence has more than one subordinate clause, it usually wants to be two sentences.

**One idea per sentence. New information in every sentence.** A sentence that only restates the previous one is cut.

**Paragraphs** run three to six sentences in most professional writing, and hold one movement of the argument. Balance them; a two-line paragraph next to a fifteen-line one signals that the long one has not been worked.

## Punctuation

**Full stop.** Ends a sentence — the completion of one idea.

**Semicolon.** Joins clauses that are each complete but bear on one another, the second often introducing a new element. Underused in professional writing, and useful for holding a two-part statement together without a heavy conjunction.

**Comma.** Separates like elements in a list, and unlike ones — appositives, parenthetical clauses. Used before coordinating conjunctions joining independent clauses. In British and Canadian practice the serial comma is optional; be consistent within a document. Never separate a subject from its verb.

**Colon.** Announces an explanation, a list, or a consequence, and often marks a causal relation. Also introduces direct speech or a quotation, followed by quotation marks. Does not take a capital after it unless what follows is a complete quoted sentence.

**Question mark.** Formal and official correspondence generally avoids direct questions, on the principle that an administration answers questions rather than posing them. Where a question must be raised, phrase it indirectly — *The question arises whether* · *I should be grateful to know whether* — which needs no question mark. In ordinary business email this constraint does not apply.

**Exclamation mark.** Not used in professional or official writing. It signals affect, which is precisely what such documents exclude.

**Ellipsis.** Marks an interrupted or unfinished statement. Never combined with *etc.* — use one or the other.

**Parentheses.** Isolate an explanation, a reference, or a digression.

**Dashes.** Preferred to parentheses in administrative prose, marking a less abrupt separation while doing the same work.

**Quotation marks.** Enclose a quotation, isolate a term for emphasis, mark a word used unusually, and carry titles of works and publications.

**Placement.** No punctuation mark begins a line. An opening parenthesis or quotation mark never ends a line; a closing one never begins one.

## Capitalisation

The governing principle: **limit capitals, and apply one solution consistently within a document.** Inconsistent capitalisation is more visible than either choice made consistently.

**Capitalise:**

- The first word after a full stop, and after a question or exclamation mark that ends a sentence. Where those marks fall mid-sentence, they function as commas and take lower case.
- The first word after the salutation.
- Proper nouns: people, places, rivers, seas, mountains, monuments, and adjectives forming part of a geographic proper noun.
- Titles of publications.
- Nouns of nationality denoting people — *the French* — while the adjective stays lower case: *French practice*.
- Acronyms and initialisms, generally without full stops between letters. Longer ones may take initial capital only.
- The first word of the name of an official body, association, or company.
- Institutions taken as legal persons: *the State*, *the Senate*, *the Crown*.
- Common nouns where the capital marks a specific sense — *the Revolution* for a particular one, *the Department* for a named one.
- Titles and forms of address when addressing someone directly, in the salutation, in the closing formula, and in the address block.
- Names of festivals and holidays.

**Do not capitalise:**

- After a colon.
- Days of the week and months.
- Job titles and functions in running text — *the minister announced*, *the director confirmed* — except in direct address.
- Adjectives derived from place names.

## Recurring errors

| Avoid | Prefer |
|---|---|
| Please find enclosed herewith | I enclose · Please find enclosed |
| As per your request | As you requested · Further to your request |
| Prior to · subsequent to | Before · after |
| In the event that | If |
| At this point in time | Now · currently |
| In order to | To |
| Due to the fact that | Because |
| With regard to the matter of | Regarding · on |
| Utilise | Use |
| Commence · terminate | Begin · end |
| Facilitate | Help · enable · arrange |
| Contact someone (as a verb, in formal writing) | Write to · telephone · get in touch with |
| Impact (as a verb) | Affect |
| Reach out to | Contact · write to |
| Solutioning · actioning | Resolving · acting on |
| Going forward | From now on · in future · (usually cut) |
| I would like to take this opportunity to | (cut entirely) |
| Please do not hesitate to contact me | Please let me know · I remain available |
| Kindly note that | Please note · (cut *kindly*) |
| Basis of · based off | Based on · founded on |
| Comprised of | Composed of · consisting of |
| Advise (meaning inform) | Inform · tell — reserve *advise* for giving advice |
| Revert (meaning reply) | Reply · get back to you |
| The above-mentioned · the aforesaid | This · the [thing itself] |
| Enclosed please find attached hereto | (choose one) |

**Also cut:** *just*, *actually*, *simply*, *very*, *really*, and *I think* where the sentence is your assertion anyway. Each weakens what follows and none adds information.

## Dangling modifiers

A participle opening a sentence must share its subject with the main clause. This is the most common grammatical error in closing formulas.

- Wrong: *Remaining at your disposal, please accept my best regards.* (*Remaining* attaches to *you*, not to the writer.)
- Right: *I remain at your disposal. Please accept my best regards.*
- Right: *Remaining at your disposal, I send you my best regards.*

Same test for any opening participle: *Having reviewed the file, the decision was taken* — by whom? *Having reviewed the file, I took the decision.*

## Numbers, dates, and references

- Write dates in full in formal documents: *14 March 2026*, not *14/03/26*. Numeric formats are ambiguous across conventions, and a document sent from Canada may be read anywhere.
- Give the day of the week alongside the date where a deadline is involved: *by Friday 14 March*.
- Spell out numbers one to ten in running prose; use figures for larger numbers, all measurements, money, and percentages. Be consistent within a sentence.
- Expand every acronym at first use, with the short form in parentheses, then use the short form throughout.
- Cite documents precisely: title, reference number, and date. A reference the reader cannot retrieve is not a reference.
- Where a figure is estimated or provisional, say so at the point of use, not in a footnote.

## Layout

- Multi-page documents carry page numbers, and where the convention applies, a continuation marker at the foot of each page except the last.
- Enclosures are numbered and listed at the foot of the first page.
- Margins of at least two centimetres.
- Lists earn their place when the content is genuinely enumerable — steps, conditions, options. Prose that has been chopped into bullets loses its connective reasoning and reads as a set of assertions.
- White space is structural. A dense block signals to the reader that the writer did not organise the material.

## Revision checklist

Run these in order before sending:

1. One subject only.
2. The purpose is clear from the first sentence.
3. The context is restated for a reader who does not have the file.
4. Every factual claim carries its date, figure, or reference.
5. No invented details — every placeholder is marked and flagged.
6. The conclusion names who does what, by when.
7. The register matches the relationship, in both directions.
8. No sentence merely restates another.
9. Every acronym expanded at first use.
10. Numbers, dates, and names verified against the source.
11. Closing formula matches the salutation.
12. Enclosures listed match enclosures actually attached.
