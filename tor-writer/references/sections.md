# The 30 sections — purpose, content, template, errors

Consult section by section as you write it (Stage 4). Each entry gives the purpose, what goes in it,
the table template where there is one, and the most frequent error.

## 01 — Cover

**Purpose**: entry point. The reader must know what this is without opening the document.
**Content**: letterhead of the issuing institution · full purpose statement · document type · date ·
version number · confidentiality marking if applicable.
**Common error**: a title that is not the purpose statement. The title may be short, but the full
purpose must appear on the cover.

## 02 — Table of contents

**Purpose**: navigation. Allows direct access to a section without reading the document.
**Content**: hierarchical numbering, headings and subheadings, page numbers.
**Rule**: use figures and letters for hierarchy to avoid ambiguity between levels.
**Threshold**: essential beyond 5 pages.

## 03 — Executive summary

**Purpose**: enable a decision without full reading.
**Content**: 1 to 2 pages maximum, carrying the salient facts of every section — problem,
objectives, expected results, phases of the method, main deliverables, major risks, duration, total
cost, decisive clauses.
**Rule**: **written last**, after the cost. Introduce nothing that is not in the body. Do not
comment, do not distort.
**Common error**: a summary that copies the introduction instead of synthesising the whole.

## 04 — Acronyms and abbreviations

**Purpose**: remove comprehension barriers.
**Content**: two-column table, alphabetical order. Also spell out each acronym at its first
occurrence in the text.
**Rule**: list only acronyms actually used in the document.

## 05 — List of tables and figures

**Purpose**: navigation among exhibits.
**Content**: numbered list with caption and page. Every table and figure in the body must carry a
number and a caption ("Table 1: …", "Figure 1: …").
**Threshold**: useful from 5 exhibits onwards.

## 06 — Revision and approval

**Purpose**: traceability and credibility. Shows who produced what, and whether the authorised
parties approved.

| Version | Date | Author / editor | Nature of the change |
|---------|------|-----------------|----------------------|

| Function | Name | Signature and stamp | Date |
|----------|------|---------------------|------|

**Rule**: an unapproved document binds no one and loses its contractual value.

## 07 — Summary sheet

## 08 — Purpose

**Purpose**: the thread running through the whole document. Both starting point and destination.
**Content**: one or two sentences answering "what is being done?". Must implicitly encompass every
specific objective.
**Method**: identify the need → formulate the general objective → break it into specific objectives
→ **come back and restate the purpose** so it covers them all (mandatory feedback loop).
**Calibration rule**: neither too generic (vague) nor too specific (overloaded).
**Example**: "Feasibility study for the construction of a bridge linking towns A and B."
**Common error**: conflating purpose and objectives. The purpose says *what is done*, the objectives
say *why and for what results*.

## 09 — Context and rationale

**Purpose**: make the action legitimate and unavoidable. The context describes the environment, the
rationale proves relevance.
**Minimum structure**: (1) context — the conditions in which the problem exists; (2) rationale —
demonstration that the action is necessary, aligned with strategic objectives and favourable on a
cost/benefit basis.
**Preparation tools**: PESTEL grid (internal / external × political, economic, social,
technological, ecological, legal) and SWOT. See `upstream-methods.md`.
**Common error**: recounting the organisation's history instead of demonstrating necessity. Every
paragraph of the context must set up an element of the rationale.

## 10 — Scope

**Purpose**: draw the boundary of what is possible. What is outside the scope is not owed.
**Content**: place, activity, resource, event, population, or a combination. State explicitly **what
is excluded**.
**Common error**: listing only inclusions. The sentence "the following are excluded from the scope:
…" prevents most execution disputes.

## 11 — Location

**Purpose**: location means travel, therefore cost and duration.
**Content**: every site concerned, at the finest available level of precision (city, district,
street, building, department).
**Common error**: "across the whole national territory" with no site list — impossible to budget.

## 12 — Date or duration

**Purpose**: the temporal boundary of the commitment.
**Mandatory MPR rule**: leave this section **blank** until the methodology, resources and schedule
are written. Duration is **derived** from the Gantt or PERT chart; it is not decided.
**Content**: total duration, start date (or start condition: "from the date of notification"),
execution period, working days or calendar days — state which.

## 13 — Reference framework

**Purpose**: point to the applicable legal, normative and regulatory framework without reproducing
it.
**Content**: for each reference — nature (law, decree, standard, procedure, good practice), exact
title, reference number, location within the text where relevant.
**Selection criterion**: cite a reference only if it genuinely aids understanding or will serve a
conformity check.
**Example**: citing ISO 11799 in terms of reference for fitting out an archive room avoids listing
dozens of technical requirements.
**Prohibited**: citing a reference whose existence has not been verified. Flag it as to be verified.

## 14 — Target groups

**Purpose**: identify beneficiaries **and** actors capable of blocking the work.

| Level | Actor(s) | Expected impact | Constraint to manage |
|-------|----------|-----------------|----------------------|
| Primary | | | |
| Secondary | | | |
| Tertiary | | | |

**Rule**: each step up the ladder reaches actors who can make the activity succeed or fail. The
"constraint to manage" column is the one that carries value.
**Common error**: listing target groups without drawing any methodological consequence.

## 15 — Objectives

**Purpose**: define the intended result. Two mandatory levels.

**General objective**: encompassing and specific at once — the administrative action, the domain,
and precision about what is expected. One sentence.
*Example*: "Supply of IT equipment: 3 printers, 4 scanners, 2 complete workstations and 1 router."

**Specific objectives**: the steps or components required to reach the general objective. Infinitive
verbs. Strict hierarchical relationship with the general objective.
*Example*: select the supplier · take delivery of the equipment · commission it · test it · train
the staff.

**Objective checklist** — at least three of the six:

| Element | Question |
|---------|----------|
| What | What result, what problem solved, what need met? |
| Who | Which beneficiaries, what needs, what expectations? |
| Why | What positive effects on beneficiaries or the organisation? |
| Where | What intervention scope? |
| How | What resources, methods, tools? |
| When | What deadline or milestone? |

**Common error**: specific objectives that are not steps toward the general objective but adjacent
activities. Test: their sum must produce the general objective, no more and no less.

## 16 — Added value / expected results

**Purpose**: what the organisation or the target group **gains**, objective by objective.
**Method**: sit beside each objective and look for its positive effect. The general objective yields
the overall added value; each specific objective yields its own.
**Common error**: conflating this with deliverables. A deliverable is a tangible output ("study
report"); added value is a gain ("an investment decision grounded in verified data, avoiding the
cost of rework").

## 17 — Methodology

**Purpose**: the pivot section. This is the "M" of MPR — it drives resources, duration and cost.
**Content**: the key milestones of the work process, each with its deliverables, lead times, and
start and closure conditions.

**Full template (L2–L3), 11 columns**:

| # | Programme | Action | Activity | Task | Description | Stakeholders | Start/end conditions | Lead time | Deliverables | Resources |
|---|-----------|--------|----------|------|-------------|--------------|----------------------|-----------|--------------|-----------|

**Reduced template (L0–L1), 4 to 6 columns**: # · Activity · Description · Deliverable · Resources.

**Choosing the granularity**:

| Level | Definition |
|-------|------------|
| **Programme** | Structured set of actions and projects pursuing medium- or long-term objectives |
| **Action** | Concrete implementation of one or more activities serving a specific objective |
| **Activity** | Set of interconnected tasks, planned, with resources and a schedule |
| **Task** | Specific, detailed piece of work, a component of an activity |

**Rule**: the **start and end conditions** are what will make the schedule buildable. Never omit them
at L2/L3, even if they do not appear in the final chart.
**Common error**: over-detailed descriptions inside the table. If a description exceeds three lines,
move it to an annex and keep only the summary.

## 18 — Performance indicators

See `risks-and-kpis.md` for the full chain metrics → operational KPIs → project KPI.

**Minimum template**:

| # | Performance indicator | Target value | Verification source |
|---|-----------------------|--------------|---------------------|

**Rule**: any indicator without a verification source is unusable. The source must be a document or
system actually produced by the activity (attendance sheet, minutes, system extract).

## 19 — Preventive risk analysis

See `risks-and-kpis.md` for the four-step method and the six qualification families.

**Template**:

| Elementary task | Attached risks | Rating (H/M/L) | Specific measures | Qualification |
|-----------------|----------------|----------------|-------------------|---------------|

**Closing rule**: the measures retained must be **fed back into the methodology** before costing. A
preventive risk analysis that changes nothing in the methodology has served no purpose.

## 20 — External risks

**Purpose**: record the risks that fall outside the MPR triangle — those that depend on external
actors and cannot be neutralised by the project team's method.
**Examples**: staff unavailable for interviews · slow approval of deliverables · power outages ·
late payment of advances · delays in providing information · theft of equipment outside working
hours.
**Content**: risk · likelihood · impact · party concerned · treatment (shared, transferred,
accepted).
**Discrimination rule**: if a risk depends on the method, the resources or the planning, it belongs
in section 19, not here. An internal risk parked in this section signals a defective preventive
analysis.

## 21 — Schedule

See `planning-and-costing.md` (Gantt, PERT, sequencing conditions).
**Minimum content**: sequence number · programme/action/activity/task · duration. Sequencing derives
from the start and end conditions defined in the methodology.
**Rule**: interdependent activities that follow one another sit on the same timeline; independent
ones run in parallel.
**Only after the schedule**, go back and fill in section 12 (date or duration).

## 22 — Communication plan

**Purpose**: secure information flow, coordination, engagement, expectation management and
visibility throughout the project.

| Audience | Key message | Channel | Frequency | Owner | Indicator |
|----------|-------------|---------|-----------|-------|-----------|

**Common error**: planning only outward-facing visibility and forgetting internal coordination
communication — the one whose absence actually derails projects.

## 23 — Deliverables

**Purpose**: what proves the activity was carried out. Basis for acceptance and payment.

| # | Related activity | Deliverable | Short description | Due date | Format |
|---|------------------|-------------|-------------------|----------|--------|

**Test**: "what is the tangible output of this task?" Every milestone produces at least one
deliverable.
**Common error**: deliverables without acceptance criteria. State format and expected standard
wherever acceptance could be contested.

## 24 — Responsibilities

**Purpose**: bound each party's liability. Three mandatory categories:

1. Responsibilities of the sponsor / provider
2. Responsibilities of the beneficiary or commissioning party
3. Shared responsibilities

**Common error**: stating only the provider's obligations. The commissioning party's obligations
(access to premises, a designated focal point, approval turnaround) are precisely the ones whose
breach derails projects.

## 25 — Project team

**Two possible formats**: an org chart of reporting lines, or a descriptive table.

| # | Position | Required profile | Number of posts | Role | Level of effort |
|---|----------|------------------|-----------------|------|-----------------|

**Placement**: may precede the methodology (recommended) or follow it, whichever reads better.

## 26 — Terms and conditions

**Purpose**: the essential obligations the parties accept.
**Content**: payment milestones and terms · advances · late penalties · ownership of deliverables and
data · confidentiality · discounts and complimentary services · amendment and termination conditions.
**Common error**: omitting intellectual property in the deliverables and confidentiality of data
accessed — two major sources of dispute.

## 27 — Resources

**Purpose**: derives directly from the methodology. The more precise the allocation, the more
reliable the costing.
**Categories**: human · material · property · informational resources.
(**Financial** resources belong in section 28, unless they are an asset of the project.)

| # | Item | Specifications | Quantity | Unit | Related activity |
|---|------|----------------|----------|------|------------------|

**Absolute minimum**: item, quantity, specifications. Without specifications, the provider will
deliver the cheapest thing that matches the item name.

## 28 — Cost

**Purpose**: the last link of MPR. Derived entirely from sections 17, 21 and 27.
**Before costing**: reread the whole document from the context to the resources — ideally have a more
experienced third party read it. This is the section where an error costs the most and is hardest to
correct.

| Category | Ref. | Item and specifications | Billing unit | Unit price | Quantity | Total |
|----------|------|-------------------------|--------------|------------|----------|-------|

Common billing units: person-day · person-month · unit · lump sum · day · kilometre.
**Totals to produce**: total excluding tax · applicable taxes · total including tax. State the
currency.
**Prohibited**: inventing unit prices. Flag the unit price as to be completed and supply the complete
table structure, ready to fill.

## 29 — Annexes

**Content**: plans · detailed requirements · technical specifications · exhibits · the FRAP or OKR
table · the full preventive risk analysis · form templates · CVs · any document supporting the
decision.
**Rule**: every annex is called out at least once in the body of the document.

## 30 — Bibliography

**Content**: sources of the principles, methods, data and quotations borrowed.
**Formats**: IEEE · ISO 690 · APA · Chicago. Pick one and stay with it.
