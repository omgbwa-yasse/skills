# Feedback and performance reviews

Give feedback the way a competent manager does: anchored in observable facts, never in a general
impression or a character trait. The failure this skill exists to prevent is vague feedback ("you
need to be more proactive") that says neither when, nor precisely what, nor why it matters — and
that the person therefore cannot act on.

## Before anything else: pick the depth

Match effort to the moment. Three modes:

| Mode | When | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **Rapid** | A single piece of feedback to phrase, right after the situation | One SBI block: situation, behaviour, impact, next step. In the conversation. |
| **Standard** | A scheduled 1:1 feedback moment, a mid-year check-in | A short set of SBI observations since the last one, framed for a specific conversation. |
| **Deep** | The annual or career-development review, or a difficult corrective conversation with real consequences | Full structure below: per-competency assessment drawn from a period-long SBI log, balanced recognition and improvement areas, a development plan. |

A Rapid request dressed up with a full per-competency write-up is as unhelpful as a Deep review
compressed into one vague paragraph — match the structure to what's actually being prepared.

## The SBI model (Situation – Behavior – Impact)

Base structure for any feedback, positive or corrective:

1. **Situation** — the precise context: when, where, in what circumstance. "During the March 12
   client meeting" — not "in general" or "often."
2. **Behavior** — the observable action, factual, without interpreting intent. "You interrupted three
   times" — not "you don't listen to others" (a character judgement, unverifiable, puts the person on
   the defensive).
3. **Impact** — the concrete consequence on the outcome, the team, or the relationship. "The client
   had to repeat the question twice, which came across as disorganised."
4. **Next steps** — what is expected going forward, or what should continue.

Anchoring feedback in facts rather than impressions makes it harder for recency bias or affinity
bias to distort it — an evaluator who cannot cite a specific situation generally does not have
enough to evaluate on, only an impression that needs re-examining.

**Keep a running SBI log.** Do not wait for the annual review to try to remember: note situations
throughout the period so that concrete evidence is available when writing, rather than a general
impression.

full worked competency assessment. `references/bias-checklist.md` has six specific biases to check
before finalising a Standard or Deep review — read it before any annual or career-development review.

## Preparing the annual or career-development review

1. **Gather SBI situations** from the whole period, both positive and improvement-oriented, spread
   across the full period (not just the last few weeks — that is recency bias).
2. **One assessment per competency, not a single overall judgement.** Each comment covers one precise
   area (delivery, collaboration, autonomy...), in 2 to 5 sentences: long enough for a concrete
   example and its impact, short enough to stay readable in 30 seconds. A comment that runs past a
   paragraph loses its point.
3. **Balance recognition and areas for improvement** without artificially inflating one to soften the
   other — feedback that is overly positive to mask a real performance problem makes any later
   consequence incomprehensible to the person.
4. **Share the agenda in advance.** Communicating the themes to be covered a few days ahead turns the
   review from an interrogation into a discussion — the person arrives prepared, not defensive.
5. **The development plan closes the review**, never the assessment alone. 2-3 concrete actions, each
   with an identified support (training, mentoring, a project) — not an injunction ("improve your
   leadership") with no way to get there.

## Difficult corrective feedback

**Separate the behaviour from the person.** "The report contained three costing errors," not "you're
careless" — the latter attacks identity, shuts down listening, and does not help the person correct
course.

**Ask before concluding on intent.** The observed behaviour may have a cause the manager is unaware
of (overload, missing information, a poorly communicated instruction). Ask before attributing intent.

**Name the consequence explicitly if nothing changes.** Corrective feedback that never states what
happens absent correction lets the person underestimate the seriousness.

**Where this feeds next.** Feedback describes and corrects a behaviour; it is not the vehicle for a
consequential HR decision (promotion, role change, dismissal) that itself needs to be argued and
approved. When the review points toward that kind of decision, build the case with `decision-memo` —
the SBI log gathered here is exactly the evidence base that memo needs to cite.

## The non-negotiables

**No assessment without a citable concrete example.** "Lacks leadership" without a specific situation
is not defensible if the person pushes back — and weakens confidence in the whole review.

**Never a judgement of character.** Describe the conduct and the consequence, never the person ("you
are disorganised" → "the last three deliverables were submitted after the agreed deadline").

**Consistency across a team's reviews.** Check that comparable behaviours receive comparable
assessments from one person to the next — perceived inconsistency is the leading cause of a review
being contested.

## Output

- **In the conversation** — a one-off piece of feedback to phrase, preparation for a difficult
  conversation.
- **`.md`** — a full review document (per-competency assessment, development plan) to keep in the HR
  file.

`assets/table-templates.md` has ready-to-copy tables for the SBI log, per-competency assessment, and
development plan.

Whatever the format, a Standard or Deep review always contains: a concrete SBI example for every
assessment made, a stated balance of recognition and improvement areas, and — for Deep — a
development plan with named support.

## Working with what the user gives you

**They give you an outcome with no situation attached** ("she's not a team player"). Do not write the
feedback from that framing — ask for, or infer from context, at least one specific situation; a
character label with no SBI behind it is exactly the failure mode this skill exists to prevent.

**They want to vent before the actual review gets written.** Let the venting happen in the
conversation, then extract only what survives the SBI test — the specific, citable situations —
before drafting. Frustration is real information about impact, but it is not itself a fact to put in
the review.

**They already have a running log of observations.** Use it as the evidence base directly rather than
asking the person to redescribe the period from memory; check it for date-clustering (everything from
the last two weeks) before treating it as representative of the full period.

## A note on limits

This skill produces feedback and review content; it is not HR or employment-law advice. Where a
review is a step in a formal process with legal weight (a performance improvement plan, grounds for
dismissal, a protected characteristic potentially in play), say so once and point to the need for HR
or legal review before the document is used for that purpose.
