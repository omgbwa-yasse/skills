# French-language Canadian résumés

A French résumé written in Canada is not an English one translated. The section names, the verb form, and the punctuation follow their own conventions, and a francophone screener in Ottawa, Gatineau, or Montreal notices when they are absent.

The rules below reflect the templates issued by Collège La Cité and the Gouvernement du Québec guide. They describe **Variant B** in full. Under Variant A, the language conventions here still apply — section names, anglicisms to avoid, French punctuation of lists — but the address, summary form, references line, and verb form follow Variant A instead. See `variants.md` before applying anything in this file.

## Verb form: infinitive, not past participle

This is the single most distinctive convention and the one most often missed by writers coming from English practice.

French Canadian résumés open each bullet with a **verb in the infinitive**:

- Accueillir et prendre les commandes des clients
- Collaborer aux activités opérationnelles et apporter un soutien au gestionnaire
- Planifier et coordonner le processus d'inscription
- Élaborer plusieurs plans de marketing

Not: *Accueilli*, *Collaboré*, *Planifié*.

The infinitive reads as a description of the function rather than a claim about a completed action, which is the register French administrative writing expects. It also sidesteps gender agreement on the participle, which is a practical benefit.

The past participle is not wrong and appears in private-sector and consulting résumés, particularly ones written by people who work primarily in English, where it reads as more results-oriented. Variant A permits either form. Variant B requires the infinitive, which is what every institutional template in French Canada uses.

Whichever form the variant sets, hold it across the entire document. Mixing the two is wrong under both.

Use the same infinitive form for current and past roles. French résumés do not shift tense by recency the way English ones do.

## Punctuation of bullet lists

French list convention: each item ends with a semicolon, the final item with a period.

```
- Recevoir et traiter les paiements en espèces, par carte de crédit ou par débit ;
- Nettoyer les tables de travail, les armoires et les appareils ;
- Sortir les déchets et nettoyer les poubelles de la cuisine.
```

Note the space before the semicolon — French typography puts a thin space before high punctuation marks (`;` `:` `!` `?` `»`). Use a non-breaking space so the mark does not wrap to the next line.

Dropping terminal punctuation entirely is also acceptable and increasingly common. What is not acceptable is inconsistency across the document.

## Section headings

Standard French Canadian section names, with the English equivalents they do not map to word for word:

| French | Notes |
|---|---|
| Sommaire des qualifications | A bulleted qualification list, not a prose summary — see below |
| Compétences | Skills, grouped by domain |
| Études, Formation, or Éducation | *Études* and *Formation* read as more idiomatic than *Éducation*, which is a calque |
| Autres formations | Short courses, certificates, workshops, under Études |
| Expérience de travail or Expériences professionnelles | The standard heading |
| Historique de travail or Historique d'emploi | Used when the entries are reduced to a bare list, as in a skills-based résumé |
| Réalisations | Projects and accomplishments |
| Références | See below |

## Sommaire des qualifications

The French convention differs from the English professional summary in form, not just language. Where an English résumé opens with three to five lines of prose, the French one opens with **four to six bulleted statements**, written impersonally in the third person:

```
SOMMAIRE DES QUALIFICATIONS

- Détient une maîtrise en administration des affaires de l'Université d'Ottawa
- Plus de 12 ans d'expérience, dont 5 reliées aux finalités de l'emploi
- Maîtrise la suite Microsoft Office
- Bilingue dans les deux langues officielles
- Permis de conduire valide
```

Note the register: *Détient*, *Maîtrise* — third person, no subject pronoun. Never *Je détiens*.

Each bullet carries one verifiable fact. The section functions as a checklist the screener can tick against the posting, which is exactly how it is read.

The prose summary in first person, standard in English Canadian practice, is not wrong in French but reads as an anglicism to some screeners. This bulleted form is what Variant B prescribes. Variant A uses the prose summary in either language.

## Bilingualism

In Ottawa–Gatineau, bilingualism is a qualification, not a language skill buried at the bottom. The La Cité templates place **Bilingue dans les deux langues officielles** either as a standalone line directly under the contact block or as a bullet in the Sommaire des qualifications.

Do the same whenever the target region or employer is bilingual. Burying it under a "Langues" heading on page two wastes the strongest differentiator a bilingual candidate has in the National Capital Region.

Where proficiency is uneven, say so plainly: *français (langue maternelle), anglais (niveau professionnel)*. If a Public Service Commission profile has been tested, give it.

## Education entries

- In progress: state the expected completion date explicitly — *Date prévue d'obtention du diplôme : avril 2026*
- **Autres formations** collects short courses with issuing body and date: first aid, CPR, workplace safety, crisis intervention, software certificates. In fields where these are screened for, they are worth their own subsection rather than being folded into skills

## Contact block

Variant B carries the full address including postal code, as the Québec templates do. Variant A gives city and province only.

The reasoning behind each: francophone institutional employers still expect the full address and occasionally read its absence as evasive, while pan-Canadian practice has moved away from it to reduce location-based screening and limit exposure of personal data. If the person wants a middle position under either variant, city, province, and postal code without the street line is defensible.

## References

The La Cité templates end with *Références : seront fournies sur demande* or *Références disponibles sur demande*. Job Bank advises against the line on the grounds that it is assumed and wastes space.

Variant B keeps it. Variant A omits it. Under both, never list the referees themselves on the résumé.

## Continuation across pages

When a section runs past the page break, French Canadian practice repeats the heading with *(suite)*:

```
HISTORIQUE DE TRAVAIL (suite)
```

This is a genuine readability convention, not decoration — it tells a screener holding page two what they are looking at. Apply it to any section that spans a break.

## Anglicisms to avoid

| Avoid | Prefer |
|---|---|
| Éducation (as a section heading) | Études, Formation |
| Aplication, opportunité | Candidature, possibilité |
| Support (as a noun for help) | Soutien, appui |
| Compléter un formulaire | Remplir un formulaire |
| Adresser un problème | Traiter, régler un problème |
| Position (for a job) | Poste |
| Assumer (for taking on) | Assumer is fine for responsibility; use prendre en charge for tasks |

## A caution about institutional templates

The La Cité templates are teaching aids aimed at students and recent graduates. They model structure and register correctly, and they model achievement writing poorly: every bullet in them is a duty statement, none carries a number, and none says what changed.

Follow their structure and their register. Do not follow their bullets. A candidate with real experience should quantify, and the infinitive form does not prevent it:

- Template level: *Créer des tableaux dans Excel pour compiler des statistiques*
- Better: *Automatiser la compilation des statistiques mensuelles dans Excel, remplaçant deux jours de saisie manuelle par une extraction planifiée*
