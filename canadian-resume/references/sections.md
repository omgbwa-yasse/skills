# Section-by-section guidance

## Name and tagline

Name at the top, largest text on the page. Below it, the target job title or a short professional tagline. This orients the screener in one second and tells them which pile the résumé belongs in.

Match the tagline to the posting where possible. Applying to a posting titled "Records Analyst" with a tagline reading "Information Management Professional" forces the screener to do a translation they may not bother with.

Do not write the word "Résumé" or "Curriculum Vitae" at the top. Everyone knows what the document is.

## Contact block

Include:
- Location. Under Variant A: city and province only (Ottawa, ON), which is current pan-Canadian practice and slightly reduces the risk of location-based screening. Under Variant B: the full address with postal code, which francophone institutional employers still expect. See `variants.md`.
- One phone number, the one that will actually be answered
- A professional email address. firstname.lastname@ is fine; a nickname from 2009 is not
- LinkedIn URL, if the profile is current and consistent with the résumé
- Portfolio, GitHub, or personal site, when the work is relevant to the job

Add when relevant to the posting:
- Bilingualism, as its own line directly under the contact block, whenever the region or employer is bilingual. In the National Capital Region this is a qualification rather than a language skill, and burying it on page two wastes the strongest differentiator a bilingual candidate has
- Work authorization status, if the posting screens on it
- Driver's licence class, for roles that require driving
- Professional association membership or licence number, for regulated professions

Omit: photo, date of birth, marital status, dependants, nationality, Social Insurance Number, and any government identifier.

## Professional summary

The form depends on the variant.

**Variant A** — three to five lines of prose. Not an "objective" in the old sense: an objective states what the candidate wants, and the employer does not care yet. A summary states what the candidate brings. First person is acceptable here and nowhere else.

**Variant B** — a *Sommaire des qualifications*: four to six bulleted statements, impersonal, each carrying one verifiable fact. A career objective of one or two sentences is also permitted under this variant, either in place of the sommaire or following it. See `french-canadian.md` for the register.

Under both, the content is the same. What changes is whether it is delivered as prose or as a checklist.

What belongs in it:
- Years of experience and field
- Two or three specific competencies that match the posting
- One concrete distinguishing fact — a credential, a scale, an unusual combination
- Language proficiency, when the role is bilingual

First person is acceptable here and nowhere else. It reads warmer than the alternative and gives the document a voice.

Avoid: "results-driven professional", "proven track record", "dynamic team player", "passionate about excellence". These phrases appear on every résumé and carry no information.

## Skills

A compact block of grouped, concrete skills. Group by category with a bold label, then list. Six to nine lines.

List things that can be verified or demonstrated: software, languages, standards, methods, certifications, equipment. Avoid unverifiable character claims — "excellent communicator", "detail-oriented" — which cost space and persuade no one. Demonstrate those in the experience bullets instead.

Where proficiency varies meaningfully, say so: "Excel (advanced: pivot tables, macros)", "Spanish (conversational)".

Use the exact terminology from the posting. If the posting says "EDRMS", write EDRMS even if the person would normally say "document management system".

## Work experience

For each role:

```
Job title, Employer, City   |   Start – End (month and year, or year alone if consistent)
- five to seven bullets, most relevant first
```

Rules:
- Reverse chronological within the section
- Be consistent about date precision across all entries; mixing "2019 – 2023" with "March 2019 – June 2023" looks careless
- Include internships, co-op placements, contract work, and self-employment. Volunteer work goes here only if it is directly relevant, otherwise in its own section
- Do not state reasons for leaving
- For roles more than fifteen years old, compress into a single "Earlier experience" line or drop them

Employment gaps: do not draw attention to them, and do not invent filler. Using years rather than months quietly absorbs gaps under a year. A gap spent studying, caregiving, or building something can be listed as an entry with a plain title. Address longer gaps in the covering letter, not the résumé.

Foreign employers: add a short descriptor if the name means nothing in Canada — "Green Light Group SARL (systems integrator, 40 staff), Yaoundé, Cameroon".

## Education

Reverse chronological. For each: credential, field, institution, city, and completion year.

- Include the graduation date. Omitting it reads as concealment
- For an in-progress credential, give the expected completion date explicitly, on its own line if the format allows: *Expected completion: April 2026* / *Date prévue d'obtention du diplôme : avril 2026*. A credential listed with an open end date and no expected completion reads as abandoned
- List relevant coursework only when the person is a recent graduate and the coursework maps to the posting
- Include academic honours and scholarships if they are notable
- Collect short courses, workshops, and certificates under a subheading — **Autres formations** in French — with the issuing body and date. First aid, CPR, workplace safety, crisis intervention, and software certificates belong here. In fields that screen for them, they justify their own line rather than being folded into skills
- Move this section above work experience only for recent graduates whose education is their strongest asset

**Credentials earned outside Canada** need help. State the credential as awarded, and add a Canadian equivalency assessment if one exists (WES, ICAS, IQAS, CES). Do not translate a foreign degree into a Canadian title that was not awarded — writing "Master's degree" for something assessed as equivalent to a bachelor's is a misrepresentation. If no assessment has been done and the target field is regulated, flag this to the person: it may be the single highest-value action they can take, more than any résumé edit.

## Certifications, licences, memberships

Separate section when there are several, or folded into education when there are one or two. Include issuing body and year, and expiry where it matters. Professional association membership signals commitment to a field and is worth its line.

## Projects, publications, volunteer work

Include only when they earn their space — that is, when they demonstrate something the work history does not. A portfolio project matters for a developer with thin employment history and is noise for one with ten years at named employers.

Publications belong on a non-academic résumé only when they establish authority in the target domain. Two books in the field, listed with ISBNs, does that. A dozen conference abstracts does not, outside academia.

Volunteer work is worth including when it shows leadership, sector knowledge, or skills the paid history lacks.

Personal interests are a variant question rather than a matter of taste. Variant A omits them unless they bear directly on the posting. Variant B permits them as a way of showing personality. Under either, an interest that could attract a judgment — anything touching politics, religion, or health — stays off the page.

## References

Never put referee names and contact details on the résumé, under either variant.

The trailing line differs. **Variant A** omits it: the availability of references is assumed and the line costs space. **Variant B** keeps it — *Références disponibles sur demande* — where its absence would read as an oversight to a francophone institutional screener.

Either way, prepare a separate one-page sheet with three referees: name, title, organization, relationship, phone, email.

Ask each referee for permission first, and tell them what job the candidate is applying for so they are not caught unprepared.
