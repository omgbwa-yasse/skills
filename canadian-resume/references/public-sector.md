# Public-sector applications

Government hiring in Canada runs on rules the private-sector template does not anticipate. A résumé that would win a private-sector interview can be screened out mechanically by a federal process, and vice versa.

## Federal (Government of Canada)

### Essential qualifications drive everything

A federal job poster lists **essential qualifications** — education, experience, and sometimes occupational certification — and **asset qualifications**. Screening is pass or fail against the essentials. A candidate who plainly holds a qualification but does not demonstrate it in writing is screened out, and the board is not permitted to infer it.

The application usually asks the candidate to answer each essential qualification in its own text box. Treat these as the primary document and the résumé as supporting evidence. For each:

- Restate the qualification in the candidate's own words
- Give a specific example with dates, the employer, and the candidate's own role in it
- Say what the candidate personally did, not what the team did
- Give scope: how many, how much, how long, for whom

Write these in full sentences, not bullets. Length is not the enemy here; vagueness is.

### Experience is counted in months

Federal posters routinely specify "recent" (usually within the last five years) and "significant" experience (a defined depth or duration). Make dates explicit and unambiguous so a screener can count months without guessing. This is one context where month-and-year precision beats year alone.

### Official languages

Positions are designated English essential, French essential, bilingual, or either/or. Bilingual positions carry a three-letter profile covering reading, writing, and oral interaction, each rated A, B, or C — BBB and CBC are the common ones.

If the candidate has been tested by the Public Service Commission, state the profile and the test dates. If not, describe proficiency plainly ("French: native; English: professional working proficiency") and expect testing during the process. Do not claim a level that has not been assessed.

### Security clearance

Most federal positions require **Reliability status**; some require **Secret** or **Top Secret**. Eligibility depends on verifiable background, and higher levels generally require a documented residency history in Canada covering the preceding five to ten years. A candidate who cannot yet meet the residency requirement should target Reliability-level roles and employers who can start the process, rather than spending effort on positions that will stall at the clearance stage.

State an existing clearance and its level on the résumé — it is a genuine competitive advantage and reduces the employer's onboarding cost. Do not state a clearance that has lapsed without saying so.

### Areas of selection

Posters restrict who may apply by geography, by employment status (open to the public versus internal to the public service), and sometimes by employment equity group. Check this before investing effort in an application.

## Staffing and consulting firms serving government

Firms that hold federal contracts screen against the categories and years-of-experience thresholds written into the contract vehicle. Their screeners are matching a résumé to a grid. This is the one context where a longer, more granular résumé helps: list technologies, standards, and project scope in detail, because the grid asks for exactly those.

## Provincial and municipal

Less standardized than federal, but the same logic holds — published qualifications, structured screening, and a preference for candidates who mirror the poster's language. Municipal postings often require a criminal record check and, for some roles, a vulnerable sector check.

## Quebec and the Charter of the French language

For positions in Quebec, write the résumé in French unless the posting is in English. Employers subject to francization requirements screen on demonstrated ability to work in French. State French proficiency explicitly.

## NOC codes

The National Occupational Classification assigns a code to every occupation in Canada. Codes matter for immigration applications and appear in some job postings and Labour Market Impact Assessment paperwork. They do not belong on a résumé for an ordinary job application — including one looks like the résumé was written for an immigration file rather than for the employer.
