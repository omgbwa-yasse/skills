# Canadian résumé writing

A Canadian résumé is a marketing document constrained by anti-discrimination norms. Two things follow from that, and almost every rule below is a consequence of one or the other:

1. **Nothing that could bias a hiring decision belongs on it.** No photo, no age, no marital status, no religion, no political views, no country of origin, and never a Social Insurance Number. This is not a style preference. Canadian employers screen these out to protect themselves legally, and a résumé carrying them signals unfamiliarity with the market.
2. **Every line competes for eight seconds of attention.** Anything that does not help this candidate get this job is taking space from something that would.

## Before writing anything

Establish these. Ask only for what is genuinely missing and cannot be inferred from what the person has already provided.

- **The target.** A specific posting is best. A target job title plus a sector is workable. "Any job" is not — a résumé written for everyone reaches no one. If the person has no target, help them pick one before drafting.
- **Their raw material.** Existing résumé, LinkedIn, a description of their background. Whatever exists.
- **Province and sector.** Federal, provincial, municipal, and private employers screen differently. Quebec differs from the rest of Canada on language.
- **Language of the posting.** Write the résumé in the language the employer posted in. For bilingual roles in Ottawa–Gatineau, Montreal, or Moncton, prepare both versions. A French Canadian résumé follows its own conventions for verb form, section names, and the summary — read `references/french-canadian.md` before writing one, and do not simply translate an English draft.
- **Work authorization.** Citizen, permanent resident, work permit, or student permit. Many postings screen on this, and some federal roles also require security clearance eligibility.

If a posting is available, mine it before drafting. Pull the exact nouns it uses for skills, tools, and standards, and use those words rather than synonyms. An applicant tracking system matches strings, and a human screener is checking off a list.

## Choosing the variant

The published Canadian sources contradict each other on address, summary form, hobbies, the references line, French verb form, and bullet punctuation. These are two coherent doctrines aimed at different screeners, not an error in one source.

The skill therefore carries **two variants**, and the person chooses. Read `references/variants.md` for the full rule-by-rule specification of each.

- **Variant A, Standard (Job Bank)** — the pan-Canadian norm. City and province only, prose professional summary, no hobbies, no references line, parser-friendly headings. Suits government, private sector, technology, staffing firms, and any application submitted through a portal.
- **Variant B, Québec institutional** — the convention taught by Services Québec and the Franco-Ontarian colleges. Full address, bulleted *Sommaire des qualifications*, hobbies permitted, references line kept, infinitive verbs, semicolon punctuation, *(suite)* continuation headings. Suits Quebec employers, francophone institutions, and applications routed through a career service or employment counsellor.

**Ask before drafting.** Put it as one question with a recommendation attached, not as a bare menu — name the employer type, say which variant fits and why, and give the two or three differences that will actually be visible. Do not arbitrate silently: a person handed a résumé missing the references line their counsellor told them to include has no way of knowing a rule was applied on their behalf.

If they have no preference, use Variant A and name it in the delivery note.

State the variant used when handing over the file, along with any deviation the person asked for.

## Choosing the format

Three formats exist. Read `references/formats.md` for the full decision logic and the layout of each. The short version:

- **Reverse chronological** — the default. Use it when the person is staying in their field and their history has no large unexplained gaps.
- **Functional (skills-based)** — groups achievements by competency instead of by employer. Use it for career changers, people with little paid experience, or histories of unrelated jobs. It costs credibility with some screeners, who read it as concealment.
- **Combination** — skills summary on top, reverse chronological history below. The most common in practice and the safest choice when unsure.

## Structure

Sections in this order, omitting any that do not apply:

```
Name
Target job title or professional tagline
Contact block (city and province, phone, email, LinkedIn or portfolio)
Professional summary (3-5 lines)
Skills
Work experience
Education
Certifications, licences, professional memberships
Projects, publications, volunteer work (if they earn their space)
```

The opening section takes one of two forms depending on the variant: a prose professional summary under Variant A, a bulleted **Sommaire des qualifications** of four to six impersonal statements under Variant B. Under either, in the National Capital Region, bilingualism belongs near the top rather than buried under languages. See `references/variants.md` and `references/french-canadian.md`.

`references/sections.md` gives the rules and worked examples for each section, including what goes in the contact block and how to handle education completed outside Canada. Read it before drafting.

## Writing the bullets

This is where most résumés fail. A bullet that describes a duty tells the employer what the job was; a bullet that describes an accomplishment tells them what this person did with it.

**Formula:** action verb + what you did + scope or result.

Vague statements like "responsible for improving efficiencies and cost savings" give the employer nothing. Compare:

- Weak: Responsible for the document management system.
- Better: Administered the document management system for a 40-person department.
- Strong: Migrated 12,000 records into a new document management system, cutting average retrieval time from 15 minutes to under 2.

Quantify wherever a real number exists. Volume, dollars, headcount, percentage, frequency, time saved. Never invent one — a fabricated metric collapses in the interview.

Cap each role at five to seven bullets, front-loading the most relevant. Lead each with a verb in the past tense, present tense for the current role. `references/action-verbs.md` has a verb bank organized by function, plus the anti-patterns to avoid.

Write impersonally. Drop "I", "me", and "my" everywhere except the professional summary under Variant A, where first person is acceptable and often warmer than the alternative. Variant B is impersonal throughout, including the summary.

In French, Variant B requires the infinitive. Variant A permits either the infinitive or the past participle, chosen once and held across the whole document. Mixing the two forms is wrong under both variants.

## What to leave out

These are the exclusions Canadian sources are explicit about, and the ones foreign-trained candidates most often get wrong:

| Leave out | Why |
|---|---|
| Photo | Not the norm in Canada; invites bias and distracts from content |
| Date of birth, age, marital status, dependants | Protected grounds under human rights legislation |
| Religion, political affiliation, ethnicity, health status | Protected grounds |
| Social Insurance Number | Identity theft risk; no legitimate reason to appear |
| Reasons for leaving previous jobs | Adds nothing positive; raises questions |
| Reference names and contact details | Supply on a separate sheet when asked; get permission first |
| Full street address | City and province is enough and is now standard |
| Hobbies unrelated to the job | Invites judgment; include only when genuinely relevant |

The first six rows hold under both variants and are not negotiable. The last two — the address and the hobbies — are where the sources part company: Variant A omits both, Variant B keeps them. Apply whichever variant was chosen rather than deciding on the person's behalf.

## Length and recency

One to two pages. Two is normal for anyone with more than a few years of experience; three is defensible only for academic CVs, senior public-sector applications, and regulated professions where a full publication or project list is expected.

Emphasize recent work. Summarize or cut anything more than fifteen years old — a single line under "Earlier experience" handles a long history without eating the page.

## Public-sector applications

Federal, provincial, and municipal applications follow rules that break the private-sector template: essential-qualification narratives, security clearance, official language proficiency levels, and NOC codes. Read `references/public-sector.md` whenever the target is a government employer or a firm staffing government contracts.

## Machine readability

Most mid-size and large Canadian employers parse résumés before a human sees them. The defensive rules are simple and cost nothing: single column, no tables, no text boxes, no headers or footers carrying real content, no images, standard section headings ("Work experience", not "Where I've Made an Impact"), and a common font. Submit .docx unless the posting asks for PDF. `assets/layout.md` gives the exact formatting specification.

## Producing the file

Use the `docx` skill to generate the document. `assets/layout.md` specifies margins, type sizes, spacing, and heading treatment. Render the result to an image and look at it before delivering — layout defects that are invisible in the source are obvious on the page.

Deliver one file per target role. Someone applying to three kinds of position needs three résumés, not one that gestures at all three. Someone applying to both a federal department and a francophone institution needs one file per variant as well — the doctrines cannot be satisfied simultaneously in a single document, and attempting it produces something that reads as inconsistent to both.

## Reviewing an existing résumé

When the request is to audit rather than write, work through `references/review-checklist.md` and report findings by severity. Lead with the things that would get the résumé discarded, not with typography. Be specific about what is wrong and what to write instead — "quantify your achievements" is advice the person has already heard and cannot act on. Rewrite two or three of their actual bullets to show the difference.

## A note on honesty

Everything on a Canadian résumé must be true and verifiable. Employers check. Inflating a title, stretching dates to cover a gap, or claiming a credential not held is grounds for dismissal after hiring, not just rejection before it. When someone's history has a genuine weakness — a gap, a foreign credential without Canadian equivalency, a job they left quickly — the answer is framing and placement, never invention. Say so plainly if asked to write something untrue.
