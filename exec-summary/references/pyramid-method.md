# The pyramid principle, in detail

Barbara Minto's method, expanded beyond the one-page application in SKILL.md.

## The core inversion

Most source documents are written (and most people naturally think) inductively: evidence first,
building up to a conclusion. The pyramid principle asks for the opposite order in the summary:
conclusion first, then the evidence that supports it — deductive presentation of what was reached
inductively. This is not just a stylistic preference; it matches how a busy reader actually consumes
a document (skim the top, decide whether to read on) rather than how a writer naturally produces one.

## The MECE test for supporting points

Each cluster of supporting points should be Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive relative to
the conclusion — no overlap between points, and together they cover the real basis for the
conclusion without gaps that would leave a skeptical reader unconvinced. In practice for a one-page
summary: if two of the three supporting points are really saying the same thing in different words,
merge them; if the conclusion rests on a fourth consideration not mentioned in any of the three
points, the summary is missing something load-bearing, not just detail.

## The "so what" governing thought

Every level of the pyramid should answer "so what?" for the level above it. The conclusion answers
"so what does this document mean?" Each supporting point answers "so what specifically makes that
true?" A point that doesn't clearly answer that question for the conclusion above it belongs to a
different pyramid, or doesn't belong in the summary at all.

## Grouping logic: three common patterns

- **Argument structure** (most common for a recommendation): reasons why the conclusion holds,
  grouped by type of reason (financial, operational, strategic).
- **Time structure** (for a narrative or a status document): what happened, in a compressed sequence,
  still opening with where things stand now, not where they started.
- **Structural/component breakdown** (for a document about a system or organisation): the conclusion
  about the whole, then the few components that explain it.

Pick one grouping logic per summary — mixing them (starting with reasons, sliding into a timeline
partway through) is what makes a summary feel disorganised even when every individual sentence is
clear.

## Titles that carry the argument

A well-built pyramid should be readable from its titles alone — the conclusion title plus the
supporting-point titles should tell the whole story even if no one reads the sentences underneath. As
a check: read only the bolded/titled lines of a draft summary out loud. If they don't form a coherent
mini-narrative on their own, the structure underneath probably isn't as clean as it feels while
writing it.

## Where source documents resist the method

Some documents genuinely don't have a single conclusion — a status report with mixed news, a
document presenting several unrelated findings. In that case, per SKILL.md's guidance on Deep mode
with multiple sources: state that plainly rather than forcing a false single conclusion, and organise
the "supporting points" as parallel findings instead of a single argument's support.
