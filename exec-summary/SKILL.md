# Executive summary

Summarise the way a practitioner applies the pyramid principle (Barbara Minto): start with the
answer, then support it — never the other way round. The failure to avoid: a summary that shortens
the document without restructuring it, keeping the original's chronological or thematic order —
forcing the busy reader to read to the end to find the conclusion, exactly what an executive summary
is meant to prevent.

## Before anything else: pick the depth

Match effort to the source material. Three modes:

| Mode | When | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **Rapid** | A short document or a single clear finding to relay | A 2-3 sentence conclusion-first summary. In the conversation. |
| **Standard** | A full report, document, or article to condense for a decision-maker | Full one-page structure: conclusion, 2-3 supporting points, implication, pointer to source. |
| **Deep** | Several source documents that need synthesising into one summary, or a summary where the source materials disagree with each other | Standard plus an explicit note on where sources converge or conflict, and which source each key point draws from. |

## The pyramid principle

**The conclusion first.** A reader who reads only the first sentence should have the single most
important piece of information. Never build the summary as a narrative that arrives at its
conclusion after presenting the evidence — the busy decision-maker does not read in that order.

**Supporting arguments grouped into a small number of clusters**, each backing the conclusion — three
clusters at most keeps the summary readable. Group by the nature of the argument (facts,
implications, risks) rather than following the source document's order.

**A title that states a conclusion, not a topic.** "The market is growing but our share is shrinking
fastest in the premium segment," rather than "Market analysis" — a reader who reads only the titles
should walk away with the essence of the story.

logics, and how to handle a source document that doesn't have a single conclusion.
`references/examples.md` has three worked summaries: a market report, a technical report for a
non-technical audience, and a Deep-mode multi-source synthesis.

## One-page structure

1. **The conclusion or the answer**, in one to two sentences — what the document ultimately says.
2. **2 to 3 key points that support it**, each with the fact or data point it rests on. No more than
   three: beyond that, the summary stops being a summary.
3. **What follows from it** — an implication, a decision to make, a risk to watch, depending on the
   nature of the source document. A summary that says nothing about what to do with it has only done
   half the job.
4. **A pointer back to the source document** for anyone who wants to go deeper — the summary does not
   replace the document, it is the entry point for whoever does not have time to read it all.

Keep it to one page. If the content resists that constraint, it is often a sign there are several
distinct messages to handle separately rather than one document that is too dense.

## Method

1. **Identify the question the document answers**, before summarising its content — a document
   without an implicit question is rare; naming it structures everything that follows.
2. **Extract, don't paraphrase line by line.** Reread the document noting the facts and conclusions
   that directly support the answer to that question; set aside context, methodological detail, and
   digressions, however interesting.
3. **Rebuild in pyramid order**, not in the source document's order — this reorganisation is the step
   that distinguishes a real executive summary from a plain shortening.
4. **Check that no figure or claim was altered** in the compression — the summary must stay faithful
   to the source document, never a rewording that betrays its nuance or accuracy.

## The non-negotiables

**The conclusion appears before the evidence, always.** This is the simplest test of a good
executive summary: the first sentence must be enough for a reader who stops there.

**No invented figure or misleadingly rounded number.** Compression never justifies an approximation
that changes the meaning of a data point from the source document.

**The summary states what follows, not just what the document says.** A purely descriptive summary,
with no implication or recommendation when the document calls for one, leaves the reader to work out
the "so what?" themselves — that is the job the summary was supposed to do.

## Output

- **In the conversation** — the summary itself, in nearly all cases: this is a short format, meant to
  be read immediately.
- **`.md`** — if the user wants to circulate it as a separate attachment from the source document.

`assets/table-templates.md` has a ready-to-copy one-page skeleton and, for Deep mode, a
multi-source convergence table.

Whatever the length, a summary always contains: the conclusion in the first sentence, and a pointer
back to the source for anyone who wants the detail.

## Working with what the user gives you

**They give you a document with no clear conclusion of its own** (raw notes, a data dump). Don't
force a false conclusion — state the strongest pattern the material actually supports, and flag that
the source itself doesn't resolve to a single clean answer.

**They ask for a summary of something you only have a partial excerpt of.** Say so explicitly rather
than summarising the excerpt as if it were the whole document — a partial summary presented as
complete misleads exactly the busy reader this skill is meant to serve.

**They want the summary to make a specific point** they already believe. Summarise what the source
actually supports; if it doesn't support the point they want, say that plainly rather than shading the
extraction toward their preferred conclusion.

## A note on limits

This skill compresses and restructures existing material; it does not verify the underlying facts of
the source document. Where the source itself is of uncertain reliability, say so once rather than
lending it unearned authority through a clean summary format.
