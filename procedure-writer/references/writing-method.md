# Writing method

## Gathering the real process

When the user hasn't yet clearly described the activity, ask questions in this order — stop as soon
as the picture is clear enough to write:

1. **The trigger.** What starts this activity? An event, a date, a request received.
2. **The sequence.** What are the steps, in the order they actually happen — not the ideal order.
3. **The branches.** Where is there a choice, an exception, a special case? This is usually where
   generic procedures fail: they describe the nominal case and leave the exception untreated.
4. **The output.** What proves the activity finished correctly — a record, a sign-off, a deliverable.
5. **The roles.** Who does what at each step; who has the final say in case of disagreement or an
   anomaly.

Don't ask about what the user clearly doesn't know yet (a process still being designed): in that
case, propose a logical sequence and invite them to correct it rather than questioning into a vacuum.

## Process sheet

Standard outline, to adapt to the level of detail retained (see `proportionality.md`):

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| Title and purpose | Process name, one sentence on what it produces and for whom |
| Pilot | The function responsible for the process running well (not necessarily who executes it) |
| Input data | What triggers or feeds the process, and where it comes from |
| Output data | What the process produces, and its recipient |
| Resources | Human, material, and informational resources needed |
| Interactions | Upstream processes (supply the inputs) and downstream ones (receive the outputs) |
| Indicators | What measures the process's performance, with a target |
| Risks | What could make the process fail; point to `risk-analysis` if an in-depth analysis is needed |
| Associated documented information | Related procedures, instructions, records |

The PRS format (Pilot / Resources / Support) is a common way to present the first three rows together
in a flowchart or a turtle diagram.

## Structure of a standard procedure

1. **Purpose** — one sentence: what the procedure covers.
2. **Scope** — where it applies and where it doesn't; explicit exclusions avoid grey areas.
3. **Reference documents** — standards, upstream procedures, forms used.
4. **Definitions** — only ambiguous or trade-specific terms.
5. **Process** — the sequence, in a table (step / actor / action / document produced) or a flowchart
   if there are many branches.
6. **Special cases and exceptions** — do not omit them; this is the section most often missing and
   most often flagged in audits.
7. **Records produced** — what, by whom, where kept, for how long.
8. **Version history.**

## Frequent pitfalls

**The over-detailed procedure.** If the level of detail describes how to tighten a screw, that's no
longer a procedure but a work instruction — separate them. An over-detailed procedure becomes
obsolete at the first minor change and no one updates it.

**The vague role.** "The quality manager or their delegate" with no statement of who decides if
neither is available. Name a backup function from the outset.

**No exception cases.** A procedure that only covers the nominal case triggers untracked ad hoc
decisions the moment a real case deviates — exactly what an audit flags.

**Copy-pasting a sector template.** A template found online or reused from another organisation
rarely describes the real practice. Always have the content validated by the person who performs the
activity before distribution.
