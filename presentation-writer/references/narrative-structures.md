# Narrative structures by type

For each type, the outline lists sections in the order they should appear. The slide count is
indicative; merge or split depending on the actual content available.

## Project pitch (5-8 slides)

1. **Hook** — the problem or opportunity, in one punchy, verifiable sentence.
2. **What exists / what's missing** — the contrast that justifies the project.
3. **The proposal** — what is being done, in one sentence.
4. **How** — 2-3 points, not the full methodological detail.
5. **What it produces** — a concrete result, quantified if possible.
6. **What it takes to get there** — resources or the decision requested.
7. **Close** — restate the central message, an explicit call to action.

No long context slide: the hook does the context work by framing it as a problem.

## Funding dossier / advocacy (12-20 slides)

1. Hook — the problem in the target audience's own terms (not the organisation's internal terms).
2. Context and stakes — scale, data, what makes the topic urgent now.
3. What has been tried / what's missing — legitimacy of the proposed intervention.
4. The proposal — general objective, expected results.
5. Approach / method — how, kept legible out loud (diagram rather than text).
6. Evidence of capability — experience, team, partnerships, prior results if relevant.
7. Budget and what each line funds — never a total alone, always the breakdown.
8. **The explicit ask** — amount, nature of the commitment sought, deadline. Never leave it implied.
9. Expected impact and how it will be measured (indicators).
10. Summary timeline.
11. Close — central message, contact details, a concrete next step for the audience.
12+ Reserve appendices: budget detail, answers to expected objections, references.

## Report-back / progress report (10-15 slides)

1. Recap of the original objective — one sentence, to reframe an audience that doesn't have the file
   in mind.
2. What was planned vs. what was done — never one without the other.
3. Results achieved, with indicators.
4. Variances and their causes — presented factually, neither minimised nor dramatised.
5. What was learned or adjusted along the way.
6. Next steps.
7. Close — the ask, if the report-back has one (validating a redirection, a renewal).

Here, the ask comes at the end — the audience must first judge performance before being asked for
something.

## Leadership committee presentation (6-10 slides)

1. The decision to be made, stated on the very first slide — never at the end. A leadership
   committee reads the conclusion before the argument; giving it upfront avoids misunderstandings.
2. Minimal context — only what the committee doesn't already know.
3. Options considered, with pros/cons — even with a recommended option, show it was compared against
   alternatives.
4. Recommendation and why.
5. Impact (budget, risk, timeline, resources).
6. Exactly what is being asked of the committee — validate, arbitrate between options, take note.

Reserve slides in appendix: financial detail, risk analysis, answers to anticipated objections.

## Choosing the opening

Three possible openings, chosen based on the audience — never "today I'm going to talk to you
about..." which opens nothing:

- **The striking figure** — a verifiable fact that immediately frames the stakes.
- **The structuring question** — a question the whole presentation answers.
- **The decision recap** — for a committee, state upfront what is being asked (see above).
