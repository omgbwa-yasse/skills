# Visual content

## Choosing the form based on the message

| The message is... | Use | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| A comparison (before/after, us/competitors, option A/B) | Bar chart, two-column table | Prose text |
| An evolution over time | Line chart, area chart | A table of raw figures by period |
| A breakdown (budget, headcount, causes) | Stacked bar chart, or pie chart if few categories | A pie chart with more than 5 slices |
| A sequence, a process | Flowchart, timeline | A dense numbered bullet list |
| A structure, an organisation | Org chart, architecture diagram | Textual description |
| A single figure to highlight | A large isolated number, with one line of context | Burying the figure in a sentence |

As soon as a data chart is needed (bars, curves, numeric comparisons), read the `dataviz` skill
before designing it: palette, form, and legibility in light and dark are covered there in detail — do
not improvise colours or pick a chart type at random.

## Density per slide

- **6 lines maximum** of visible text per slide, each line short.
- **A single chart or diagram** per slide, never two competing for attention.
- The slide title states the message, not the topic: "Training budget has doubled," not "Training
  budget."
- Appendix slides (held in reserve for questions) can be denser: they are only shown if needed, not
  read aloud in the main flow.

## Consistency with the source document

When the presentation derives from a TDR, a project dossier, or a risk report already produced
(`tor-writer`, `risk-analysis`), reuse the source document's figures, objective titles, and
vocabulary exactly. A discrepancy between the filed document and the projected slides undermines the
credibility of the whole thing, even when the gap is unintentional.

## Speaker note

Every slide delivered includes, in addition to the on-screen content, a "to say" line: what the
speaker explains out loud that the slide does not show. This is what distinguishes a presentation
outline from a document sliced into pieces.
